INTRODUCTION

On October 30, the Nasdaq fell 2.86% and the semiconductor index dropped 7.2% in a single U.S. session. Reuters cited fears that AI investment was burning through cash as a driver of the selloff. Intel's 28.6% plunge added to the pressure.1 In the trading sessions that followed, the market repeatedly returned to the same question.

Strong sales at AI suppliers do not establish that their customers will earn an adequate return on investment. That is why the fiber-optic boom of 2000 is so often invoked.

  • Back then Telecom operators borrowed heavily to lay fiber on the assumption that internet usage would grow without limit.
  • Two years later Roughly $2 trillion in telecom market capitalization and 500,000 jobs had disappeared, according to testimony by the chair of the U.S. Federal Communications Commission (FCC).9
  • Yet Internet usage kept growing. The losses fell on network builders and their investors.

This report therefore separates three distinct outcomes.

AI technology succeedsAdoption grows and services gain traction≠Capital is recoveredInfrastructure investors recover their outlay and cost of capital≠Shareholders profitInvestors make money on the companies' shares

We turn three conditions behind the 2000 bust, identified by Kiwoom Securities, into questions to ask of both eras, then finish with a monitoring checklist.10

  1. 1Was capacity built without customers?
  2. 2Was it financed at high rates on weak credit?
  3. 3Can the investment pay back within its useful life?

Cash left after investment
turns negative for the first time

In Q3, three of the four Big Tech companies spent more on equipment than they generated in operating cash. Demand had not turned down; investment was simply accelerating faster than cash generation. Unlike the new telecom entrants of 2000, these companies still fund most of their spending internally.

Combined Q3 free cash flow for the four companies was −$26.4bn—the first negative quarterly reading since at least 2017 on comparable data, using the same definition as Eugene Investment & Securities.13All four companies still produced positive operating cash flow. The deficit emerged only after deducting capital spending.

021996~2002

How did the fiber-optic boom
unravel in 2000?

Internet traffic kept increasing. The problem was that demand expectations overshot reality by roughly sixfold, while supply expanded faster than actual usage.

First, an important distinction: usage means the volume of internet traffic, while orders refer to telecom operators' spending on equipment. Traffic kept rising, but equipment orders fell sharply from 2001 onward. It is those orders that matter most to Korean suppliers (Section 07).

STAGE 1 · EXPECTATIONS'Internet traffic doubles every 100 days'

  • April 1998 A U.S. Department of Commerce report repeated an estimate from UUNET, a WorldCom subsidiary: 'Internet traffic doubles every 100 days.'26
  • October 1998 Research by Coffman and Odlyzko measured annual growth closer to 100%. The truly explosive increase had occurred in 1995–1996.27
  • July 2000 An FCC staff study traced the '100 days' figure to a single industry white paper.28
  • September 2002 The president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston concluded that demand forecasts had been off by roughly a factor of six.29

STAGE 2 · INVESTMENTCapacity doubled in five years; twenty long-haul carriers in one city

  • Carrier capex Listed U.S. telecom service providers' capex peaked at $121bn in 2000 and fell by more than half to $49bn in 2002.18
  • Route mileageU.S. long-haul fiber routes grew from 143,000 miles in 1996 to 320,000 miles in 2000. Chicago and Minneapolis each had twenty long-haul operators, compared with about five per city in 1990.18
  • Risks disclosed by companies themselves Level 3 warned in its 1999 filings that it expected to incur substantial capex before meaningful revenue and that competitors' capacity could materially depress prices.32

STAGE 3 · CAPACITY SURGECapacity increased without digging another trench

Dense wavelength-division multiplexing (DWDM) sent multiple wavelengths of light through a single fiber. Upgrading the equipment at each end could multiply the capacity of cables already in the ground, while 30operators raced to lay new routes. The FCC chairman cited estimates that network capacity had grown by as much as a factor of 500.9

The Richmond Fed identified three causes of the long-haul glut: excessive entry early in the industry's development; larger-than-expected gains in per-strand capacity; and, most importantly, demand that fell short of expectations.30 Supply exceeded expectations even as demand failed to meet them.

STAGE 4 · PRICE COLLAPSEMore than 90% in two to three years

  • By July 2000 The Band-X bandwidth-exchange price index stood about 63% below its October 1998 level. The researcher cautioned that the index was not representative of the entire market.28
  • By mid-2002TeleGeography estimated that wholesale circuit prices had fallen more than 90% on most routes within two to three years. In October 2001, some trans-Pacific cable capacity was offered for resale at prices far below the estimated cost of construction.33
  • The regulator's explanation The FCC chair testified that a brutal price war had driven down industry revenues and those of individual firms.9

STAGE 5 · DEBT AND BANKRUPTCYJunk-bond-funded operators failed first

  • Credit spreads surge Telecom junk-bond spreads widened by 1,129 basis points (11.29 percentage points) between January 2000 and August 2001.34
  • Defaults concentrate Telecom accounted for more than half the value of U.S. and Canadian high-yield defaults in 2001–2002.35
  • Debt overhang The FCC chair estimated industry debt at 'around $1 trillion by some estimates'.9
Detail: defaults and the order of failuresDefault shares by data provider, troubled bank loans, bankruptcy chronology and revenues inflated by reciprocal transactions
  • Default concentration Telecom accounted for 55% of the $63.6bn of U.S. and Canadian high-yield defaults in 2001, and 52% of the $96.9bn total in 2002.35
  • Bank lending A 2002 shared national credit review of large syndicated loans classified 27% of telecom and cable credit commitments as criticized credits, up from 3.9% a year earlier.36

Some firms also overstated financial performance. In Q2 2001, reciprocal purchases of similar value with other carriers accounted for 91% of Global Crossing's sales of long-term indefeasible rights of use (IRUs). Excluding these transactions, the company's claimed adjusted EBITDA would have been −$43m, not +$472m. The SEC sanctioned the company for inadequate disclosure.39 Qwest improperly recognized more than $3.8bn in revenue through reciprocal arrangements and other transactions and paid a $250m fraud settlement.40 Reciprocal deals concealed the revenue damage caused by collapsing prices.

WHAT COULD INVESTORS HAVE KNOWN?Warning signs already public by 2000

  • 'Doubling every 100 days' was one company's projection; academic measurements put annual traffic growth closer to 100% (October 1998 paper and November 2000 follow-up).27, 41
  • Level 3's 1999 disclosures warned of major capex before meaningful revenue and possible price pressure from competitors' capacity.
  • A November 2000 Bank for International Settlements (BIS) report found that telecom received roughly a quarter of global syndicated loans in January–September 2000. European operators' 3G spectrum payments accounted for a substantial share.42

Default rates, bankruptcies and accounting scandals became evident later. Some early warnings were publicly available before the wave of failures.

SETTING THE RECORD STRAIGHT'Only 2.7% of installed fiber was being used'

  • The source This is not an official government statistic. Citing a Merrill Lynch report from April 2001, the Dallas Fed noted that long-haul backbone utilization had fallen from 15% in 1988 to below 3% in April 2001—and cautioned that the figure did not account for peak-hour usage.43
  • What it measures Average utilization of equipped, or 'lit,' capacity—not the share of all fiber strands installed in the ground. This was Merrill Lynch's description as reported in the trade press; telecom executives disputed the claim.44
  • Official data The FCC's published series ended with 1998 data. In 1996, 53.1% of AT&T's fiber strands were equipped and in service.45

Odlyzko attributed the bust to overinvestment, not a slowdown in traffic growth.46SK Securities argues that demand weakened first in both the dot-com bust and the global financial crisis, but 47the fiber-optic industry collapsed even as internet usage kept growing. Strong usage alone does not establish that a repeat of 2000 is impossible.

Kiwoom Securities' assessment

‘Not yet’If the cycle falters, the trigger is more likely to be a counterparty credit event—a customer failing to pay—than an immediate collapse in equipment values across the board.10A fresh look at official disclosures likewise finds insufficient evidence of a systemic, 2000-style collapse.

That does not mean profitability has been established.

  • Even with contracts, low pricing can mean poor returns.
  • Companies can service their debt while shareholders still lose if equipment values fall and repeated equity issuance dilutes existing holders.
  • Even uninterrupted access to financing cannot prevent returns from deteriorating as payback periods lengthen.

0420002026

The weakest link is
the customer's credit

In 2000, equipment makers extended credit to customers to keep equipment orders flowing. Today, financing exposure is spread across guarantees, leases and joint ventures.

2000Vendors financed customers to sell equipment

  • LucentCustomer-financing commitments had reached $8.1bn by September 2000, which Lucent said was often a prerequisite for winning contracts or bidding. The company recognized approximately $1.8bn and $765m in customer-financing losses in FY2001 and FY2002, respectively.52
  • Nortel At year-end 2000, undrawn customer-financing commitments reached approximately $4.1bn; in January–September 2001, Nortel reserved about $1.07bn against customer receivables and financing losses.53
  • Cisco Customer loan commitments were about $1.9bn in July 2001. When demand suddenly fell, Cisco wrote down $2.25bn in inventory.54

Equipment vendors were selling to cash-constrained competitive local exchange carriers (CLECs). Aggregate capex by these customers fell nearly 80% from its 2000 peak by 2002.18 When customers failed, vendor loans became vendor losses.

2026Guarantees, leases and joint ventures replace vendor loans

  • Nvidia lease guarantee Disclosed in August: Nvidia provided a guarantee with a cumulative cap of $105bn for a 20-year lease under which SB Energy would lease facilities in Portsmouth, Ohio, to an OpenAI affiliate, covering roughly 4.25GW of IT load. This is not a guarantee that activates only at lease maturity: Nvidia may have to cover shortfalls during the term if OpenAI becomes unable to pay (Table 3).55
  • Meta's Hyperion project In October 2025, a joint venture owned 80% by funds managed by Blue Owl and 20% by Meta agreed to develop a data-center complex costing approximately $27bn. Meta guaranteed a capped portion of residual value over its initial sixteen years of operations.56
  • SpaceXOn October 26, SpaceX disclosed a commitment for roughly $40bn in GPU finance leases with an Apollo-managed entity. Each equipment tranche carries a term of approximately five to 5.25 years. Customer credit risk rests with the lessor and its investors.57

The end customers fall into two broad groups: Big Tech, with $676.8bn of trailing-12-month operating cash flow, and a handful of AI labs dependent on capital raises and IPO proceeds. The weaker link is the second group.

  • Kiwoom Securities The ability of a few major buyers, including Anthropic, to meet commitments depends on IPO proceeds and guarantees from hyperscalers.10
  • DS Investment & Securities As Anthropic expands its direct model—leasing chips and installing them in dedicated facilities—the AI developer bears the risk of paying lease charges even when construction is delayed.58

In its amended registration statement on November 2, Anthropic described the mismatch itself. Most revenue is usage-based rather than locked into long-term contracts, while compute and infrastructure commitments run for years. Many of its largest customers can also reduce or stop spending at any time.59 Revenue depends on usage. The bills are committed for years.

  • Revenue and operating resultsRevenue totaled $33.2bn for January–September. Anthropic posted $970m in operating profit in Q2, but reported a $570m operating loss in Q3 after incurring $5bn in costly short-term compute expenses and $2bn in stock-based charitable donation matching (a noncash expense).59
More from the filingLeases not yet commenced, funding sources, collateralized borrowing, and Broadcom equipment leases
  • Leases not yet commenced These increased from $50.2bn at year-end 2025 to $451.2bn as of September 30. Most are expected to commence in 2026–2028, with a weighted-average term of roughly ten years. They are included in the commitment total above.
  • Sources of funding Operating cash flow was $6.6bn in January–September, while financing activities provided $110.7bn, largely from issuances of redeemable convertible preferred stock.
  • Secured borrowing Anthropic increased its revolving credit facility from $3.25bn to $15bn in September. Substantially all assets secure the facility; collateral and guarantees may be released if at least two of three rating agencies assign investment-grade ratings.59
  • Broadcom equipment leases Anthropic will pay approximately $161.2bn, excluding interest, for TPU equipment acquired under five-year leases. In October, Broadcom transferred some rights and obligations under equipment leases to a new special-purpose vehicle (SPV). Anthropic stated that the transfer did not increase its total lease payments.59 Reuters reported that Bank of America, Citi, Morgan Stanley and Blackstone had committed $63bn in financing, with Broadcom providing residual-value support to lenders on some equipment.60 Hana Securities noted that splitting the exposure among suppliers, SPVs, lenders and bondholders does not eliminate the underlying risk.25

In 2000, equipment makers bore most of their customers' credit risk. Today, the exposure is shared among equipment suppliers (Nvidia and Broadcom), lease vehicles and funds, the banks and private-capital managers financing them, and lessees themselves. The structure is examined in greater detail in Research No. 12.

0520002026

Efficiency echoes 2000.
The power grid changes the equation.

In 2000, an equipment upgrade could multiply capacity on cables already in the ground. Today, projects can stall because power is unavailable. Yet there is a parallel: technology can rapidly expand effective supply.

WHAT IS DIFFERENT

  • Who funds the buildout Then, startup telecom operators relied on IPO proceeds and debt. Today, cash-generative Big Tech finances most investment.
  • The bottleneck Then, endpoint equipment upgrades multiplied capacity, and grid interconnection was not the primary constraint it is today. Now, access to power is the bottleneck.
  • Credit quality Then, telecom accounted for over half of high-yield defaults. Today, the largest investors borrow at investment-grade rates—Microsoft is rated Aaa.2, 35

WHAT RHYMES

  • Rapid growth in usage Google's monthly AI token volume rose more than 300-fold in two years, according to figures cited by Kiwoom Securities. Internet usage also surged in 2000.10
  • Efficiency expands supply Just as DWDM expanded capacity per fiber strand, a given GPU can produce more tokens, while the cost of generating an equivalent answer is falling rapidly (Table 6).10
  • A surge of new entrants Then, Chicago and Minneapolis each had twenty long-haul telecom operators.18 Now, neoclouds (emerging GPU-rental providers) and former Bitcoin miners that have pivoted into data-center leasing have entered in force.10

Power and siting constraints cut both ways: they may prevent a 2000-style surge of excess capacity, but they also delay the payback on equipment already purchased.

Whether regulatory concerns result in actual restrictions remains uncertain. Demand indicators, however, are strong: CBRE reports a 1.4% vacancy rate across eight major U.S. markets and 80.4% preleasing for capacity under construction in H1 2026. Hana Securities argues that such constraints will affect commissioning dates and investment returns more than demand itself.25

Three different prices must be tracked.

If the cost of providing compute falls faster than usage-based prices, providers can cut prices and preserve margins. If prices fall faster than costs, customers have greater leverage to renegotiate rental rates.Whether GPU rents break, as wholesale circuit prices did in 2000, is the key to the third question.

Counterarguments—and
four possible paths

The case for why this time is different is substantial. But each counterargument depends on evidence that has yet to be verified.

Yuanta Securities

A cash-flow recovery, as in 2019

Following the 2017–2018 cloud investment cycle, FCF rose in 2019 to $38.3bn at Microsoft and $31.0bn at Alphabet. Yuanta Securities argues that cash recovery can begin once capex growth has passed its peak.23

WHAT TO CHECKThe same firm still forecasts negative combined FCF for the four companies in H1 2027 (Section 01).

SK Securities

Bubbles burst when end demand falters

In SK Securities' reading, dot-com demand and then housing prices weakened first; vendor financing and accounting manipulation amplified the damage. It sees funding concerns today but no evidence yet of weakening AI demand.47

WHAT TO CHECKAs Section 02 showed, the fiber-optic industry collapsed while usage kept rising. The rate of supply growth and pricing must be examined together.

iM Securities

Incremental returns already look positive

For the three major cloud providers, each additional dollar of depreciation was accompanied by $2.10 of incremental cloud operating profit in Q3 2026, up from $0.50 a year earlier. That is above the upper end of iM Securities' 1.6–2.0× benchmark, although Amazon alone stood at 1.5×. Even if the roughly $200bn FY2027 funding shortfall across five companies including Oracle (dividends included) were fully financed with debt, net debt/EBITDA would remain below 1.2× for all four Big Tech firms; Oracle alone would reach 4.4×.62

WHAT TO CHECKDepreciation follows capital spending with a lag, so the ratio can look favorable while investment is accelerating. Return on invested capital (ROIC) has fallen close to the cost of capital. iM Securities also cautions that funding capacity and the profitability of AI services must be evaluated separately.62

Daishin Securities

Credit stress may remain company-specific

Daishin assigns a 60% base-case probability to AI credit problems remaining confined to individual companies. It would watch for broader contagion if the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield exceeds 5.30% or high-yield spreads reach 450bp.63

CURRENT READINGSU.S. 10-year yield: 4.90% (Nov. 4); high-yield spread: 340bp (Nov. 3).64, 65

There is also a more cautious calculation. Yuanta Securities finds that justifying $1.1tn of capex in 2027 would require revenue above market expectations even if the assumed required return fell to 5% (Figure 14).66

Equipment stocks fell before capital spending contracted. Telecom equipment investment peaked in 2000 and began to decline in 2001, but the equipment index had already peaked alongside the Nasdaq in March 2000.31, 43

  • The current shortageMemory supply remains tight, and 2027 output of high-bandwidth memory (HBM) has already been subject to advance contracting (Kiwoom Securities).10But as Cisco demonstrated in 2001, a sudden stop in orders can turn accumulated inventories into losses.54
  • Growth rates Both Yuanta Securities and Hana Securities expect capex growth to slow next year.23, 24But if spending moves from 100 to 200 to 260, the growth rate slows from 100% to 30% even as the amount spent continues to rise.

That makes total spending and growth rates insufficient on their own. Four additional indicators deserve attention.

How that spending is financed matters too. The more customers rely on debt and guarantees, the more their suppliers' orders depend on financing conditions.A low probability of default is not the same as an attractive expected return for shares in the supply chain.

Internet usage kept rising through the fiber-optic bust of 2000. What undermined investor payback was capacity growing faster than usage, falling prices and the cost of debt. The scale of AI contracts or growth in token consumption alone cannot establish investment success.

The warning presently visible in public data is neocloud debt. Industry-wide uncontracted construction and exposure to weaker customers remain unquantified.From here, track not just AI usage, but whether that usage generates enough cash to recover invested capital, cover its cost and leave something for shareholders.

Glossary
Free cash flow (FCF)
Operating cash flow less cash spent on capital assets. This report calculates FCF as operating cash flow minus purchases of property and equipment.
Operating cash flow
Net cash generated or used in core operations, distinct from accounting profit.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. Different from operating profit after depreciation or cash generated by operations.
Finance lease
An arrangement economically similar to purchasing an asset while paying in installments through lease payments. The full asset cost is not necessarily reflected immediately in cash capex.
Lease not yet commenced
A lease contract signed but not yet in effect. The obligation may be disclosed in footnotes before it is recognized on the balance sheet.
DWDM
Dense wavelength-division multiplexing: technology that increases a fiber strand's capacity by transmitting multiple wavelengths of light simultaneously.
IRU
Indefeasible right of use: a long-term contract granting use of fiber capacity, often for 10–20 years.
Syndicated loan
A large loan provided by a group of banks.
High-yield debt / junk bonds
Corporate debt rated below investment grade, generally carrying higher yields.
Spread / basis point (bp)
The extra yield above a benchmark such as government bonds. One basis point equals 0.01 percentage point.
SOFR
Secured Overnight Financing Rate, a short-term U.S. dollar benchmark. Floating-rate loans typically charge SOFR plus a credit spread.
CDS
Credit default swap: a derivative that transfers default risk. A higher CDS premium generally indicates a higher market-implied risk of default.
Residual-value guarantee
A commitment to cover a shortfall if resale or re-leasing proceeds fall below a contracted amount. The trigger depends on the contract; Nvidia's guarantee is linked to tenant insolvency or nonpayment of rent.
Special-purpose vehicle (SPV)
A legal entity established for a specific transaction, such as buying and leasing equipment or raising debt. It can separate designated assets and liabilities from those of its sponsor, subject to accounting and legal rules.
Take-or-pay contract
An agreement requiring payment for a committed amount even if the customer does not use all the capacity.
Remaining performance obligations (RPO)
The value of contracted obligations not yet recognized as revenue, typically to be earned over several years.
Neocloud
An emerging cloud provider focused on renting GPU compute capacity, such as CoreWeave, Nebius or IREN.
Big Tech / hyperscaler
A technology company operating data centers at enormous scale. The four examined here are Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta.
IT 1MW · GW
One megawatt of power consumed by IT servers in a data center (1GW = 1,000MW). Kiwoom estimates roughly $26m of investment in GPU equipment per IT MW.
Counterparty credit risk
The risk that a contractual counterparty cannot make payments when due. Even a signed contract is no guarantee of cash collection.
Token
A unit of text or other content processed or generated by an AI model; token throughput is one indicator of usage.
HBM
High-bandwidth memory: high-throughput DRAM placed next to AI accelerators to move data rapidly.
