EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

  1. The governor's figures check out. The BIS won REER averaged 101.9 from January 1994 onward; its August reading was 89.9.
  2. A rapid recovery, but still below the long-run mean. The index rose 11.7% over three months to 92.7 in September, 9% below its post-1994 average but close to its five-year mean.
  3. Earnings forecasts have already moved with the exchange rate. In two weeks, Hana Securities cut its Q4 USD/KRW assumption for SK hynix from 1,495 to 1,340—virtually matching October's average of 1,340.7.
  4. Lower FX assumptions do not necessarily mean lower profit forecasts. At SK hynix, stronger DRAM pricing more than offset the currency headwind. The impact is tougher for automakers with a large won-denominated cost base.
  5. The won strengthened despite foreign equity selling. The Bank of Korea cited improved FX supply and demand from a large current account surplus, alongside interest-rate increases.

Two seemingly conflicting claims are circulating: "The won has strengthened too quickly" and "The won is still weak." Both can be true: the first describes the pace of the rally, the second its level. We need to establish two things: whether the governor's figures hold up, and whether equity analysts' earnings forecasts have caught up with the exchange rate. For the FX effects still embedded in third-quarter earnings, see Investment Note No. 10, “The Bill for a Stronger Won”.2

01REMARKS

“An average of 101, around 89 at end-August”:
the BIS data support the claim

At an October 22 press conference, Bank of Korea Governor Hyun Song Shin said that the won was still weak by historical standards, whether measured against the U.S. dollar or on an effective exchange rate basis. With the dollar particularly strong in recent years, he argued, trade-weighted measures provide a better perspective than USD/KRW alone, and cited the BIS indexes.3

DEFINITION

What is an effective exchange rate?

Rather than measuring the won against the dollar alone, an effective exchange rate measures it against a basket of trading-partner currencies, weighted by their importance in trade. The real effective exchange rate (REER) also adjusts for differences in price levels across economies. A higher index value indicates a stronger won on this basis.4

What the numbers mean

  • June's reading of 83.0 was the won's weakest since March 2009. Only 61 of the 393 months since 1994 recorded a weaker reading.1
  • The BIS cautions that index levels cannot tell us whether a currency is undervalued or overvalued: they show changes relative to the base year.4 Being below a historical mean tells us where the won stands, not how much further it must appreciate.

How the rally unfolded

  • Early-July acceleration: The daily NEER rose 5.0% between July 1 and the first policy-rate hike on July 16, then gained another 6.2% between the July 16 and August 27 hikes.7, 8
  • Brief pullback around the September FOMC: The index slipped from 92.6 on September 14 to 90.2 on September 18 before recovering.
  • Exceptional speed: Since 1994, only the rebounds following the Asian financial crisis and the global financial crisis produced faster three-month gains. This time, there was no comparable shock.1 The governor characterized the unusually high USD/KRW rate in early summer as an aberration and its rapid decline as a reversal of that distortion.3
04EARNINGS FX

Q4 exchange-rate assumptions have nearly caught up with reality

Even if today's exchange rate persists through year-end, the gap versus those embedded in earnings forecasts is only 1–2%.

The forecasts have adjusted. On October 6, Hana Securities assumed USD/KRW at 1,495 for SK hynix in Q4; by October 19 it had cut that figure to 1,340—a KRW 155, or 10.4%, reduction in just 13 days.12, 14 That makes it difficult to argue that the recent won appreciation has yet to be reflected in earnings estimates.

05FOREIGN FLOWS & THE WON

Why the won can strengthen even as foreign investors sell Korean stocks

Equities are only one channel through which dollars flow into and out of Korea. This year, a substantial current account surplus has helped improve the supply-demand balance in the FX market.

Forces supporting the won

  • Current account surplus: USD 279.2bn in January–August, roughly four times the year-earlier figure. The governor expects the full-year surplus to exceed the BOK's August projection of USD 450bn.3, 18
  • BOK's assessment: Improved FX supply and demand, supported by the current account surplus, and a shift toward tighter monetary policy (rate hikes) have helped strengthen the won.3
  • Exporter dollar selling: Exporters supplied substantial dollar-selling orders early on October 20, according to Eugene Investment & Securities.10

Forces weighing on the won

  • Foreign equity outflows: Foreign investors have sold a net KRW 199tn of KOSPI-listed shares this year, including KRW 173tn in SK hynix and Samsung Electronics alone.19
  • Korean investment overseas: In August, residents increased their foreign portfolio securities holdings by USD 16.6bn and foreign direct investment by USD 6.2bn.18, 20

But not every won received from selling Korean shares is exchanged for dollars, just as not every dollar of current account surplus is sold in the onshore FX market. These totals cannot simply be netted to determine which force prevailed. iM Securities described the disconnect as two markets seeing different stories: equity investors focused on AI-related concerns, while FX traders priced in the continuing surplus.21

  • Over the five sessions, net selling totaled KRW 344.3bn. Selling pressure eased markedly from the Oct 1–16 period (net outflows of KRW 17.7tn over 10 sessions), but calling this a sustained turn to net buying would be premature.
  • Foreigners ended an 18-session selling streak in SK hynix (a cumulative KRW 16.2tn) with net purchases on October 21; the stock ranked second in foreign net buying again on October 22.28, 29, 30
  • On October 23, foreign investors sold nearly KRW 1tn of shares again.26

  • Even within Asia, Singapore's dollar (+16%) and China's yuan (+10%) stand above their own historical means. The won's weakness cannot be attributed to dollar strength alone.

COUNTERARGUMENTPOTENTIAL HEADWINDS

Three forces that could slow the won's appreciation

CAPITAL FLOWS

Surplus proceeds can leave Korea again

Korean residents' overseas investment rose by USD 22.8bn in August alone. Continued overseas buying by households and pension funds could reduce the share of surplus proceeds converted into won.18, 20

SEASONALITY

Year-end dividend payments create dollar demand

The BOK identified substantial year-end dividend payments as a downside risk to the current account surplus.18

DOLLAR & OIL

U.S. inflation and oil prices are rising

October's flash U.S. PMI showed companies raising their selling prices at the fastest pace since August 2022.31 Brent crude settled at USD 116.72 a barrel on October 23.32 Renewed U.S. inflation concerns could support the dollar, while persistently expensive oil would raise Korea's import bill.

None of these factors proves that the won's rally is over. If appreciation slows, the gap between Q4's realized average exchange rate and the FX assumptions in earnings models could narrow further.

For investors, the FX rate embedded in earnings forecasts matters more than the won's position relative to a historical mean. At that point, product pricing, input costs and currency hedges—not FX alone—will distinguish the earnings winners from the losers.

APPENDIX · A mechanical USD/KRW equivalent for an effective-rate move

This is neither a forecast nor a target. The governor also declined to endorse a specific level when asked about USD/KRW in the mid-1,200s on October 23.5

For the daily nominal effective exchange rate to rise from 94.83 (Oct 20) to its post-1994 nominal mean of 101.99, the won would need to appreciate another 7.6% against its trading-partner currency basket. If every other currency's USD exchange rate were held fixed, that would translate mechanically from USD/KRW 1,328.1 (Oct 20 closing rate) to roughly 1,235.7, 11 By the same arithmetic, USD/KRW 1,340 corresponds to an illustrative NEER of about 94.0, and 1,360 to about 92.6. In reality, the dollar and other trading-partner currencies move too. These conversions are only intended to convey the order of magnitude.

TERMS · GLOSSARY
Effective exchange rate
A trade-weighted index of the won's value against a basket of trading-partner currencies, not just USD. A higher reading means a stronger won.
Nominal vs. real
The nominal index captures exchange rates; the real index also adjusts for relative inflation. The BIS publishes the real measure monthly and the nominal measure both daily and monthly.
BIS broad index
The Bank for International Settlements' effective exchange rate series covering 64 economies, with 2020=100. Trade weights are updated on a rolling three-year basis.
Market-average reference rate
The published USD/KRW rate based on the weighted average of transactions in Seoul's FX market on the preceding business day. This note calculates quarterly averages using the BOK ECOS market-average reference-rate series.
Bottom income quintile
The lowest-income 20% of households when households are divided into five equally sized groups by income.
Current account balance
The balance of trade in goods and services plus cross-border income, including interest and dividends. A surplus can improve FX supply conditions, but is not equivalent, dollar for dollar, to onshore currency conversion or net dollar inflows.
Exporter dollar offers (“nego” flows)
Offers to sell dollars in the FX market by exporters converting their dollar receipts into won.
H.10
New York noon FX rates published by the U.S. Federal Reserve, useful for comparing multiple currencies at a common observation time.
PMI
A business survey-based economic activity indicator; a reading above 50 signals expansion.
