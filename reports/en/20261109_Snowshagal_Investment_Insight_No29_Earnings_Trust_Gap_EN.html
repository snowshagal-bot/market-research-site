SUMMARY · Five Key Points

  1. Earnings rose. Share prices fell. Since the June 22 peak, the KOSPI's 12-month forward EPS rose 32.9%, but the index fell 44.5%. Mathematically, the entire decline reflects PER multiple compression—from 8.5x to 3.54x. Why investors cut that multiple is the question this report examines.
  2. At a historical P/E, the earnings implied by today's index are barely one-third of consensus. Yuanta Securities calculates an implied earnings level 63.4% below consensus, or 73.6% below for semiconductors. That may reflect doubts about future profits—or higher interest rates and risk premia.
  3. Two core doubts, with regulation as the trigger. How long can earnings last when chips account for 79% of the 2027 profit forecast? Can the funding behind AI investment withstand stress when CoreWeave bonds yield 16.5%? U.S. state-level data-center restrictions have amplified both concerns.
  4. Foreign investors sold; U.S. credit moved alongside those flows. Foreign investors sold a net ₩10.32tn in the first week of November. Their trading moved more closely with the dollar, the won and U.S. high-yield spreads than with the KOSPI itself (Hana Securities, based on contemporaneous correlations).
  5. The signals to watch extend beyond the KOSPI. Chip orders, AI funding, yields, the dollar and foreign flows. As of Nov 9, three of the report's 13 confidence signals were met, while four awaited data.

9:03 a.m., Friday, November 6. Three minutes after the open, the KOSPI was down 8.55%. A Level 1 circuit breaker halted trading for 20 minutes, following a drop of more than 12% in the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX)in the previous U.S. session.1, 2

The index closed at 5,059.69, down 44.5% from its June 22 high of 9,114.55—nearly half its value. On November 9 it rose more than 2% intraday, only to close 0.81% lower at 5,018.85. Foreign investors sold for a seventh consecutive trading session.3, 4

The paradox is earnings. Over the same period, analysts raised their 12-month earnings expectations for KOSPI companies by 32.9%. Semiconductor results also exceeded expectations in the third-quarter reporting season.2 If profits are at record highs, why have stocks fallen so far?

01 Earnings and Price

Earnings forecasts rose 33%,
while the index fell 45%.

An index level can be expressed as earnings multiplied by the PER P/E multiple investors are willing to pay. If earnings rise while prices fall, the multiple must contract. Since June 22, multiple compression accounts for the entire decline in this arithmetic breakdown.2

Multiple compression describes the arithmetic of the decline; it does not explain why investors lowered the multiple. They may doubt the durability of earnings, demand a higher risk premium, react to rates or positioning, or respond to market flows. Sections 02–05 examine those possible drivers.

What Matters

  • A forward P/E of 3.54x is the lowest since 2001. PBR P/B (price divided by book value) is also below 1, at 0.95x.2
  • Earnings estimates rose even during the selloff. Forward 12-month EPS increased 9.0%, from 1,310 on October 28 to 1,429 on November 5. About 3 percentage points of that gain came from the monthly roll of the forward window toward the more profitable year 2027. The 32.9% gain since June also includes this mechanical effect.2
  • The discount is deeper than three weeks ago. NO.12 examined the earnings cuts implied by a KOSPI level of 5,554 on October 15. By November 6, the index had fallen another 8.9%, even as earnings forecasts climbed further.

Yuanta Securities applied the median P/E since 2004 to today's index level and backed out implied earnings of roughly ₩400tn—63.4% below the ₩1,103.7tn analyst consensus. Since 2007, this gap has generally remained within 20%, widening to around 30% even in extreme periods such as the global financial crisis.5

This is a hypothetical calculation that holds P/E at its historical level, not a direct measure of what investors actually expect earnings to be. Yuanta interprets the gap as a sign that the market distrusts the stepwise profit growth assumed in consensus forecasts. The calculation alone cannot isolate how much reflects rates or required risk premia.5

What, then, is the market questioning? Broker research points to two fundamental doubts, both sharpened by U.S. regulation.

02 Doubt One · Durability

The first doubt:
How long will these earnings last?

The market doubts the longevity of profits more than their current level. Semiconductor PER P/E multiples of 2–3x have historically appeared near cyclical earnings peaks.6

Why Investors Are Skeptical

  • Memories of cyclical peaks. Domestic semiconductor forward P/E was similarly low in Q3 2010, Q2 2014 and Q3 2018—periods when semiconductor operating earnings were at near-term peaks.6
  • Concentration risk. A shift in chip-sector forecasts moves the entire index's earnings outlook. Excluding Samsung Electronics and SK hynix, Q3 earnings missed expectations by 2.7%; 2026 operating-profit forecasts were cut in 19 of 26 sectors.2 Semiconductors also represent 52.8% of KOSPI market capitalization.6
  • Upward revisions are slowing. Weekly earnings-estimate upgrades decelerated from +10.07% to +1.70% for SK hynix and from +6.37% to +2.62% for Samsung Electronics. Upgrades have shifted toward AI compute and networking names such as AMD and Arista.7
  • Stocks have led earnings down before. After Samsung Electronics shares peaked in November 2017, January 2021 and July 2024, operating profit still rose another 9.8%, 43.5% and 33.2%, respectively. Hanwha Investment & Securities argues that equities price changes in expected future growth, rather than simply reflecting current profits.8

  • Customers have not canceled orders. SK Securities says past memory downturns typically followed this pattern: share-price peak → order cuts about three months later → memory-price declines another three months later. No order cuts have been identified since the June peak.9

03 Doubt Two · AI Credit

The second doubt:
Can AI financing hold up?

Much of Korean semiconductor profitability depends on investment in U.S. AI data centers. If builders cannot secure funding, GPU orders may be delayed—and demand for the HBM memory installed alongside those GPUs could weaken. For now, the stress is concentrated not in U.S. Big Tech generally but in newer cloud operators building data centers with borrowed money (neoclouds), notably CoreWeave.2

What the Numbers Show

  • CoreWeave's five-year CDS (default protection premium)rose from 475 bp in late May to a record 1,268bp on Nov 5, then eased to roughly 1,155bp on Nov 6. A spread of 1,000bp implies a cumulative five-year default probability of around 60%, depending on the assumed recovery rate (Daishin's conversion: roughly 63–64%).2
  • CDS spreads widened among AI suppliers. Over 30 days, Oracle rose 59bp, Broadcom 62bp and Nvidia 49bp, versus just 1bp for Microsoft.2
  • U.S. technology-sector investment-grade credit spreads rose from 117bp in early January to a year-to-date high of 159bp. Since late September, investment-grade spreads have widened faster than high-yield spreads.10
  • Operating cash cannot keep pace with capex. This year, hyperscaler capital spending is expected to increase 82%, versus only 25% growth in operating cash flow. The respective growth rates begin to converge in 2027, at 39% and 33%.10
  • CoreWeave's repayment schedule. Scheduled principal repayments for 2027 total roughly $6.2bn across several borrowings, based on Q2 filings. This is not a single bond maturity: the issue is whether cash receipts under long-term contracts align with debt repayment dates.11

But why did bond yields fall for Big Tech companies with negative free cash flow? Negative FCF does not automatically imply an inability to service debt. Big Tech has cash reserves, operating cash from established businesses, access to bond markets, and discretion over the pace of investment. Neocloud operators finance more of their infrastructure with debt and rely heavily on external funding. That distinction underpins Daishin Securities' view that hyperscalers' financing conditions have actually improved.2

The Next Test: Anthropic's IPO

  • A Nasdaq listing before Thanksgiving (November 26)is reportedly the target, with a possible offering size around $100bn.12, 13
  • Valuation expectations have been coming down. Daishin Securities estimates a revised range of $1.0tn–$1.3tn. Kyobo Securities reports that some investors are discussing valuations near $1.3tn, down from earlier expectations as high as $2tn.2, 14
  • As of November 5, the IPO price range had not been disclosed. It was still absent from the amended registration statement filed November 2.13

Kyobo Securities sees Anthropic's eventual offering price and proceeds as a test of investors' willingness to fund—and value—the AI industry.14 Hana Securities presents a counterpoint: hyperscalers' current return on invested capital (ROIC) of 18% differs from the late-1990s downturn in telecom and equipment makers, suggesting technology credit spreads could begin narrowing rather than widening further.10

04 The Trigger · Policy

Regulation amplified both doubts;
the exposure is concentrated in 2028

The November 3 midterm elections brought new governors to several states with sizable data-center pipelines. Some governors-elect pledged moratoriums on new permits or conditional pauses. Daishin Securities views the prospect of restrictions as a trigger for the selloff, sharpening concerns about both earnings durability and AI financing. But state restrictions primarily affect new approvals: most capacity due online next year is already under construction.2, 15

The actual operational impact of data-center delays may be a 2028 issue. Stocks, however, discount that risk today—and foreign investors have accounted for much of the selling.

How Heavy Was the Selling—and Why So Fast?

  • Much of the overweight has already unwound. Daishin calculates that Korea's weight in a hypothetical global portfolio rose from 6% at the beginning of the year to 10.7% on June 19 as Korean stocks surged, before falling back to 6.6% by November 6.2
  • Foreign ownership of semiconductors: 46.6%. As of November 5, the lowest level since 2013.2
  • Rules-based selling amplified the fall. Volatility-targeting funds (up to $135bn, Barclays estimate) and leveraged ETFs (about $47bn, Nomura estimate) sold mechanically. The 44.5% decline from peak to trough took 93 trading sessions, faster than in 1997 (109) or 2008 (240).2

That does not necessarily mean foreign buyers will return quickly. In past episodes when the index fell more than 25% and heavy foreign selling persisted beyond 100 sessions, foreign net selling continued over the next one to three months in 70–74% of observations. Hana Securities looks for a return no earlier than three months out; the statistic is based on qualifying trading-day observations.17

Risks That Could Delay or Reverse the Thesis

1

If earnings weaken

Order cuts, a turn lower in memory contract prices , or weaker Big Tech capex forecasts in late January. Daishin's bear case (30% probability) puts the index at 4,050–4,250. Historical selloffs exceeding 50% all involved earnings collapses.2

2

A credit event

Daishin assigns a 25% probability to CoreWeave cutting guidance and borrowing at high rates. In that case, high-yield spreads could widen and the KOSPI could test 4,800. Eugene Securities expects neocloud credit risks to persist for now.2, 6

3

If interest rates resume rising

Daishin argues that core CPI of 0.4% or more could cause markets to price additional 2027 rate hikes and push the 10-year Treasury yield back toward 5%. Foreign selling could accelerate again.2

The first risk would erode earnings themselves. The second and third would raise investors' required discount even if earnings forecasts held steady. If all three are avoided, the gap between earnings and prices could narrow as confidence returns.

Conclusion

Today's prices discount
confidence, not earnings

The KOSPI looks cheap because earnings estimates are high. Those estimates have risen 33% since June, yet earnings implied by a historical P/E stand at barely one-third of consensus. The market is questioning not the current level of profits but their durability—and the financing behind AI investment.

Two things matter: whether earnings estimates hold up, and whether the required risk premium declines. Because foreign investor flows have tended to track the dollar and U.S. credit more closely than the KOSPI, those markets deserve attention too. The investment test comes down to three questions.

  1. Are earnings still rising?No order cuts; are memory contract prices and semiconductor exports holding up?
  2. Can AI funding hold up?CoreWeave results, CDS 1,000 bp, Tech investment-grade spreads at 159bp, and Anthropic's offering
  3. Are conditions improving for foreign investors?The dollar and won, U.S. high-yield spreads, the 10-year Treasury yield at 4.8%, and foreign index futures

Methodology
  • Breaking the index into EPS and P/E: KOSPI = 12-month forward EPS × forward P/E. June 22: 9,114.55 = 1,075 × 8.48x. Nov 6: 5,059.69 = 1,429 × 3.54x (EPS as of Nov 5). EPS ratio 1.329 × P/E ratio 0.418 = 0.555, or −44.5%.
  • Earnings implied by historical P/E (Yuanta): Applying the median forward 12-month P/E since 2004 to the current index level and solving backwards for earnings. This is a hypothetical calculation holding the multiple at its historical level, not investors' actual earnings forecast. For a related explanation, see No. 12.
  • KOSPI levels at different P/E multiples (Daishin; EPS 1,429): 4x: 5,717 · 5x: 7,146 · 6x: 8,576.
  • Drawdown from the peak: 5,059.69 / 9,114.55 − 1 = −44.5% (Nov 6); 5,018.85 / 9,114.55 − 1 = −44.9% (Nov 9). Intraday low: −46.1% (Daishin).
  • Since No. 12: 5,554.22(10.15) → 5,059.69(11.6) = −8.9%.
  • Monthly roll effect in forward 12-month EPS: Of the +9.0% increase from Oct 28 to Nov 5, +5.7% came from earnings updates and +3.0% from moving the forward window into a more profitable year (Daishin).
  • Cover chart: The KOSPI uses daily closing prices. Forward EPS plots four observations cited by Daishin—June 22 (1,075), late August (1,240), Oct 28 (1,310) and Nov 5 (1,429)—with connecting line segments for illustration. No intervening daily EPS series is implied.
Historical Statistics (Conditions and Samples)
  • Drawdowns greater than 30% (Daishin; 10 episodes since 1980): Median drawdown: −44.5%. Median KOSPI returns after the trough: +31.2% over three months, +40.7% over six, and +60.3% over 12. In four episodes exceeding −50% (1997, 1998, 2001, 2008), earnings collapsed amid the Asian financial crisis, dot-com bust or global financial crisis. With just 10 observations, individual episodes materially affect the results.
  • Severe two-day declines (Daishin; 10 largest since 1990): Median 12-month return: +18.6%. In three cases where stocks fell a further 9% or more within a month (Nov 1997, May 1998, Sep 2000), earnings forecasts were also deteriorating.
  • After heavy foreign selling (Hana): The study identifies 179 qualifying trading days across four distinct episodes (2002, 2008, 2020 and 2026), requiring a drawdown of at least 25%, 120-day foreign-flow intensity ≤−150bp and over 100 days of net selling. The share of observations with a switch to foreign net buying after one and three months was 30% and 26% respectively; in other words, net selling persisted in 70–74%. The 12-month median KOSPI return (+39.3%) and positive-return frequency (94%) are calculated over trading-day observations, so the 2008 episode (119 days) carries substantial weight. Measured once from entry into each episode, 12-month returns were +1.0% (2002), −6.5% (2008) and +86.1% (2020).
  • Earnings cuts during drawdowns (Hana): When the U.S. semiconductor index fell at least 30% from its peak, S&P 500 semiconductor forward 12-month EPS forecasts fell an average 16% (2008, 2011, 2020, 2022, 2025). When Samsung Electronics shares fell at least 40%, forward 12-month net-income estimates were cut an average 34% (2008, 2022, 2025).

Glossary
12-Month Forward EPS / P/E
Consensus earnings per share over the coming 12 months, and the price investors pay per unit of that earnings forecast.
PBR⁠·ROE
Share price divided by book value, and net income divided by equity. P/B below 1x means the stock trades below its stated book value.
Market-Implied Earnings (Back-Solved)
The earnings level that would justify today's price at a historical P/E multiple. It is not a direct measure of investors' actual earnings expectations.
Investment-Grade (IG) / High-Yield (HY) Spreads
The yield premium investment-grade or speculative-grade corporate bonds offer over government debt. Wider spreads signal more expensive borrowing.
CDS
The premium paid to insure against a corporate bond default. A 1,000bp premium equals 10% of notional principal per year.
Order Cuts
Customers canceling or reducing orders already placed; often viewed as an early signal of a memory-chip downturn.
LTA
Long-term supply agreements specifying volumes and pricing terms years in advance.
Hyperscalers / Neoclouds
Large U.S. cloud operators building data centers at scale (Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, etc.); and newer GPU-rental cloud providers such as CoreWeave.
Free Cash Flow (FCF)
Cash generated by operations after capital expenditure.
bp
One-hundredth of a percentage point; 100bp equals one percentage point.
Consensus / Guidance
An aggregate of analyst estimates; and financial forecasts issued by the company itself.
Core Inflation
Inflation excluding volatile food and energy prices, used to gauge underlying price pressures.
Correlation Coefficient
A measure of how closely two variables move together (−1 to +1); it does not establish causality or which moved first.
VKOSPI
Expected future volatility embedded in KOSPI 200 options prices; higher readings generally signal more uncertainty.
Volatility-Targeting Funds
Funds designed to reduce equity exposure mechanically when market volatility rises.
Circuit Breaker
A Level 1 trading halt lasting 20 minutes after the index has declined at least 8% from the prior close for one minute.
