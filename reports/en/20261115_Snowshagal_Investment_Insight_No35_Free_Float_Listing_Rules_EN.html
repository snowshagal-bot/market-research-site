SUMMARYTHE FIVE-POINT BRIEF
  1. The Nov. 13 meeting set out a direction, not a new threshold. The president raised delisting companies with less than 10% of shares available for trading (free float). Regulators are reportedly considering tougher continued-listing rules for companies below 20%. Neither the threshold nor an effective date has been settled.
  2. The rules are much looser after a company lists. For KOSPI companies, the minimum holding by public shareholders is just 5%; large companies can qualify without meeting a percentage test at all.
  3. Truston counts 32 companies below 20%, most of them small. Its tally comprises 20 KOSPI and 12 KOSDAQ companies. But the list changes depending on whose holdings are excluded from the free-float calculation.
  4. Once a threshold is set, companies may have to release shares into the market—or leave it. In a voluntary delisting, a tender offer gives minority holders an opportunity to sell. If a company instead fails to meet the rules and is delisted, its last exchange-trading window is the seven-session final trading period.
  5. A change of control raises the same question. Under the mandatory tender-offer bill before the National Assembly, a buyer that has already acquired a majority stake could be required to purchase zero additional shares from minority holders.

On November 13, South Korea's president chaired a capital-markets policy roundtable at the Korea Exchange. The deputy prime minister for economic affairs, heads of the FSC and FSS, exchange officials, lawmakers, industry representatives and academics attended.1, 2

The focus: listed companies almost entirely owned by their controlling shareholders. Imagine 100 shares outstanding, with 95 held by the principal shareholder, family and affiliates. Just five would be available to other investors—the free float. The president cited companies where a controlling shareholder owns more than 50% but less than 10% of shares trade freely, questioning whether such companies should remain listed.1That evening, a tally identified 32 listed companies with less than 20% free float.3

The government's concern is a market structure in which one controlling owner can dictate virtually everything. Two policy avenues emerged: reconsider eligibility to remain listed, or make transfers of corporate control easier by opening the door to mergers and acquisitions. At the same meeting, the president described hostile M&A as a central mechanism for restoring market discipline.1

Both routes could lead to controlling shareholders selling stakes—or companies leaving the exchange. For minority shareholders, the question is the same: will I be able to sell for what the controlling shareholder gets?

One transaction offers an early illustration. In late October, a TPG-led buyer acquired 61% of Lotte Rental from Hotel Lotte and other sellers for KRW 59,000 a share. The mandatory tender-offer bill before the National Assembly would require a buyer of control to offer to purchase a specified amount of public shareholders' stock on the same terms. Yet under the bill's formula, TPG—having already bought more than half—would owe minority shareholders no additional purchases. TPG nevertheless launched a separate offer, at the same price, for the remaining 39%.4In other words, passage of the bill as drafted would not guarantee minority holders that opportunity in the next deal.

This report follows three questions: Could my holdings be affected? (Sections 2–3.) What happens if my company is delisted? (Section 4.) And can I sell at the same price when control changes hands? (Section 5.)

POINTS TO CONSIDER

  • The FSC chair promised a review, not a new rule. He said the agency would examine whether current distribution requirements were inadequate or insufficiently effective.5
  • This is not settled policy. EBN reported that no firm decision had been made to delist companies simply because controlling shareholders own more than half. The ownership threshold and its application to existing listings remain open questions.1
  • Both policy avenues target much the same companies. Controlling ownership is the common target. Free-float rules push owners to sell stock into the market, while hostile M&A and mandatory offers would make control easier to challenge and transfer. Truston's chief executive cited 570 listed companies where controlling stakes exceed 50% and the price-to-book ratio (P/B) is below 1x.14, 15

For how control battles translate into actual shareholder votes, see No. 26 on Hanjin KAL.

Strict at the IPO.

Korea and Tokyo define eligible shares differently, and admission rules are not the same as continued-listing rules. The percentages are not directly comparable; the point is how much Korea relaxes its requirements after admission.

POINTS TO CONSIDER

  • Controlling stakes can rise after an IPO. The Korea Exchange chair cited cases where controlling shareholders' holdings had approached 80% after listing.1
  • The percentage test barely constrains large issuers. KOSPI waives it once public shareholders hold 2 million shares. A company with 100 million shares outstanding could pass with just 2% in public hands.17
  • Compliance is assessed once a year. An issuer is designated an administrative issue the day after the annual-report filing deadline. If it still falls short the following year, delisting proceedings begin.17

Once Rules Change, Companies Have Four Paths.
Minority Shareholders Face Four Different Outcomes.

Options 1 and 2 keep a company listed; options 3 and 4 take it off the market. The distinction between those two exit routes matters most to minority holders.

POINTS TO CONSIDER

  • For controlling shareholders, waiting to be delisted might be cheaper. A voluntary exit generally requires purchasing outstanding shares through a tender offer. An involuntary delisting does not require that payout. Hanwha Investment & Securities warned that some owners might deliberately choose the cheaper, easier route (MTN). The article cautioned that failure to meet a rule alone is not evidence of intent.27
  • Market-capitalization delisting cases are currently on hold. On October 2, a court partly granted two companies' applications to halt delistings triggered by insufficient market capitalization. Its concern centered less on the numeric threshold itself than on the timing of application and procedural rights, including the right to object. The exchange subsequently suspended further delisting steps for companies below the market-cap threshold (while retaining administrative-issue designation).10 Grace periods and due-process protections will therefore be central to any new free-float standard.
  • A transfer without the final trading period has become possible. In September, the government allowed companies below the market-capitalization threshold that meet specified financial tests to transfer to KONEX without a final trading period.9 That exception is tied to the market-cap test; whether it will also apply to a new free-float rule is undecided.
  • Another route can cash out minority holders. A cash-funded comprehensive share exchange can be approved by a special shareholder resolution. An owner with around 85% can pass it alone; dissenting shareholders' stock is also transferred in exchange for cash. Critics note that the route bypasses the exchange's voluntary-delisting review.28
  • Canceling treasury shares could push a company below the line. Under the revised Commercial Act, companies generally must cancel the shares they hold in treasury.29 Consider 100 shares: the controlling owner holds 78, treasury stock is 5, and other investors own 17. Counting treasury stock as freely held yields 22 shares. Canceling the five reduces total shares to 95; the public portion becomes 17, or 17.9%, falling below a 20% threshold. If treasury shares are excluded from free float from the outset, however, the ratio rises from 17% to 17.9%.

The Case for and Against Stricter Delisting Rules

Tighten the rules
  • President: Companies should not be able to enjoy the benefits of a listing while operating as de facto monopolies controlled by insiders.5
  • Professor Kim Woo-chan, Korea University: KOSDAQ delistings amount to only 30% of new entries, versus more than 70% on Nasdaq and over 100% in Tokyo and New York. Injunctions have also delayed 39 market-cap-based delistings.30
Protect investors first
  • Industry concerns reported by MoneyToday: Tougher continued-listing requirements could conflict with protecting existing minority shareholders. Delisting removes their trading access; safeguards should accompany reform.14
  • Head of the Korea Venture Business Association: A troubled company is not the same as an undervalued one. Cheaply valued small caps can become vulnerable to opportunistic hostile takeovers; safeguards are needed.30
Japan Applied Pressure Earlier

In March 2023, the Tokyo Stock Exchange asked all Prime and Standard Market issuers to manage with greater awareness of their cost of capital and share prices. Its push drew particular attention to companies trading below P/B 1x book value.31 Delistings hit consecutive records: 94 in 2024 and 125 in 2025.32, 33 Yet almost none were forced off the exchange for falling short of listing requirements. Of 96 delistings in fiscal 2024, 95 stemmed from reorganizations—such as becoming wholly owned subsidiaries or mergers—and none from failure to meet continued-listing standards.34 Japan also has rules governing exits in restructurings. Once a bidder exceeds two-thirds ownership through a tender offer, it cannot cap the number of tendered shares it will purchase; all shares tendered must be accepted. It is not necessarily obliged to acquire shares that were never tendered.35

COUPON05MANDATORY TENDER OFFERS

50% Plus One Share—or 100%?
The Difference Is How Much Minorities Can Sell.

The rule would determine whether public shareholders can sell on the controlling shareholder's terms when ownership changes hands. Korea introduced such a regime in 1997 and abolished it amid restructuring during the 1998 Asian financial crisis.36

SHARES THE BILL WOULD REQUIRE A BUYER TO PURCHASE
50% of outstanding shares + 1 share − Shares the buyer already ownsPrior block purchasesIncludes the controlling stake acquired before the offer

In simple terms, a buyer need only reach 50% of all outstanding shares plus one. If it already bought a majority from the controlling holder, it would not have to buy any minority shares. Under the Political Affairs Committee's substitute bill, this applies when a buyer acquires at least 25% and becomes the largest shareholder. Within 15 days of the prior acquisition, it must announce a tender offer. If shareholders tender more shares than the buyer is required to purchase, acceptances are allocated pro rata among tendered shares. The law would take effect one year after promulgation.37, 38

Suppose a controlling owner holds 40%. After buying that block, the acquirer needs only another 10% of the company. Minority shareholders own the other 60%, but can sell just 10% of the company—one-sixth (16.7%) of their shares—at the same price. A 100% offer does not compel everyone to sell; it gives every willing shareholder the option to do so.

REAL-WORLD EXAMPLE: LOTTE RENTAL
  • October 30: Lexicon Korea Holdco, the TPG-led buyer, acquired 22,212,063 shares from Hotel Lotte and Busan Lotte Hotel for KRW 59,000 per share, totaling KRW 1.3105 trillion.39
  • November 6 tender-offer filing: The buyer offered the same price for all 14,097,325 remaining shares, with no cap on acceptances. The offer runs through December 3 (settlement December 7). The stated objective is delisting.4

The buyer voluntarily offered a route to sell shares that the bill's formula would have put at zero.40

THE ARGUMENTS ON BOTH SIDES

Full or equal pro-rata purchases
  • Professor Kim Woo-chan, Korea University: No other country uses a 50%-plus-one-share threshold. A study of 41 countries found no evidence that mandatory offers reduced M&A activity after adoption.6
  • Korea Corporate Governance Forum: The U.K., Germany, France, Hong Kong and Singapore require an offer for all remaining shares.41
  • Eighteen domestic and overseas asset managers: Do not give credit for pre-offer block purchases; instead, let controlling and minority holders sell pro rata on equal terms.7
Start with 50% plus one
  • President: A 100% mandatory offer would be ideal, but if the financing burden discourages takeovers altogether, minority holders may lose opportunities too.6
  • FSC capital-markets bureau director: Some EU analyses and practitioner surveys suggest adverse effects. The FSC's position is to begin with 50% plus one share, assess the results and consider broader coverage later.6, 41
  • Head of the Private Equity Fund Managers' Association: A 100% obligation could suppress buyouts by domestic private-equity funds. Equal pro-rata selling by controllers and minority holders could also reduce the controller's incentive to sell.6

At the roundtable, a show of hands favored the 50%-plus-one approach and equal pro-rata purchases over giving controlling blocks priority. Livestream comments leaned toward 100% offers. No vote totals were released.6

WHERE THE TWO POLICY TRACKS MEET

  • For roughly one in three listed companies, the bill would offer minorities zero compulsory purchases. Cha Partners Asset Management counts 756 listed companies (29.3%) with controlling stakes above 50%.36 For those companies, a change of control would not itself require a tender offer for any additional minority shares under the proposed formula.
  • If tougher float requirements force controlling shareholders to sell down, the next question is who buys their stock—and at what price. That is why the delisting routes in Section 4 and the tender-offer rules here cannot sensibly be designed in isolation.

CONCLUSIONCONCLUSION

For Minority Shareholders, the Exit Matters
More Than the Percentage.

When a threshold is announced, check three things—in this order.

  1. Start with the definition. “Free float” must be defined. Are treasury shares, affiliates' holdings and financial institutions' stakes excluded? The calculation can determine which side of the threshold your company falls on before the percentage itself matters.
  2. Then look at the exit route. If a company fails the requirement, will a tender offer or minimum exit price be mandatory? The key is whether protections will prevent route 4 in Section 4: a forced delisting without a purchase offer.
  3. Finally, ask how much can be sold on equal terms. Mandatory tender-offer rules: will the final text still credit the controller's prior purchases, or allow both controlling and public shareholders to sell the same proportion? For companies with controlling stakes above 50%, that choice could determine whether minorities get any purchase entitlement at all.

This report is not a recommendation to buy or sell. Cutoff dates and calculation methods appear in the expandable notes below.

Glossary
Free float
Shares actually available to trade after excluding holdings of the controlling shareholder, related parties and others. Exactly which holdings are excluded varies by definition.
Shareholder-distribution requirement
A continued-listing test requiring ownership to be sufficiently dispersed, assessed through the number of public shareholders and the percentage they hold.
Public shareholders / Adjusted share count
For KOSPI purposes, public shareholders exclude the largest shareholder, related parties and major holders with stakes of 10% or more. The adjusted denominator excludes government and Korea Deposit Insurance Corporation holdings, lock-up shares and other ineligible stock.
Administrative-issue designation
A formal Korea Exchange classification identifying stocks approaching delisting criteria. Unresolved deficiencies can lead to delisting proceedings.
Voluntary delisting / Final trading period
A voluntary delisting is initiated by the issuer. The final trading period is a seven-session window before delisting in which trading continues without daily price limits.
Mandatory tender offer / Prior block purchase
A takeover rule requiring the buyer of control to offer to purchase minority shares on the same terms up to a prescribed ownership threshold. A prior block purchase is the controller's stake bought before the offer; the bill would credit those shares against the obligation.
Pro-rata acceptance / Equal pro-rata treatment of prior block purchases
Pro-rata acceptance allocates purchases proportionately among tendered shares when an offer is oversubscribed; it is included in the committee bill. Equal pro-rata treatment would instead require controlling and public shareholders to sell the same proportion from the outset, without first crediting the controlling block. Eighteen asset managers proposed this alternative.
PRS (price return swap)
A contract transferring shares to a financial institution with a later cash settlement based on the difference in the share price over the agreed period.
