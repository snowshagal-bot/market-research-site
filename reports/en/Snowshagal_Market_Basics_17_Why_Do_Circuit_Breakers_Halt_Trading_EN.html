01THE EVENT & THE QUESTION

The 20 Minutes
Trading Stopped

We begin with a question again—this time about something that actually happened last week.

WARM-UPREAL-WORLD EVENT

At around 9:03 a.m. on November 6, the KOSPI had stayed more than 8% below the previous close for a full minute. What did the Korea Exchange do?

Correct answer: ③ A 20-minute halt across the KOSPI market

That's a circuit breakerA safeguard that temporarily halts trading across a market after a sharp index decline. First introduced for the KOSPI in December 1998.. The KOSPI and KOSDAQ markets are assessed separately, so the KOSDAQ had a sidecar curb, not a circuit breaker, that day. Choices ① and ② describe real safeguards too. We'll explore them in Chapter 2.

November 5–6What happened over two days

  1. 11.5

    A sell-side sidecar curb was activated in the KOSPI market at about 10:16 a.m. and in the KOSDAQ market at about 2:27 p.m. The KOSPI closed down 5.28% at 5,383.80.

  2. 11.6

    A Level 1 circuit breaker was triggered in the KOSPI market around 9:03 a.m. Three minutes later, a sell-side sidecar curb was activated on the KOSDAQ.

News reports pointed to rising concerns about AI data-center regulation after the U.S. midterms as an initial catalyst. Here, our focus is not what caused the selloff, but how the safeguards worked.

Six in 26 years. Eleven this year alone.

The earlier six came twice in 2000, on September 12, 2001—the day after the 9/11 attacks—twice in March 2020 as COVID-19 spread, and once in August 2024. This year, the first halt came on March 4; eight months later, the count had reached 11. In other words, days with intraday declines of more than 8% have become markedly more frequent.

There is a catch: the threshold has changed. The three triggers in 2000–2001 occurred under a 10% decline rule. Today's 8% threshold was introduced in 2015. These are not counts measured against identical rules.

Why does a circuit breaker
stop the market?

THE ONE-SENTENCE ANSWER
To pause trading during a steep selloff, giving investors time to reassess rather than react in a rush.
When shares plunge, sell orders can pile up before anyone has time to assess the price. The Korea Exchange likewise describes the purpose of circuit breakers as giving investors time to make considered decisions.

Safeguards affecting just one stock

Most Korean-listed common shares can trade only within ±30% of the previous day's closing price in a single session (since June 2015). Those boundaries are the daily upper price limitThe highest price a stock can reach in a trading session—generally 30% above the previous close for ordinary Korean-listed shares. and the daily lower price limitThe lowest price a stock can reach in a trading session—generally 30% below the previous close for ordinary Korean-listed shares.. Hitting either limit does not halt trading. Orders can still execute at prices within the limits if buyers and sellers can be matched.

If an individual stock moves abruptly during the day, a two-minute call auction can be triggered through a VIVolatility Interruption: a KRX mechanism that collects orders for two minutes and executes them at one auction price when a stock's price moves sharply. (see Part 9). Other stocks continue to trade as usual.

Why do sidecars watch futures?

Program tradingAutomated orders to buy or sell multiple stocks as a basket according to preset rules. involves orders to buy or sell baskets of stocks according to preset computer rules. When stock-index futuresContracts to buy or sell an underlying asset at an agreed price on a future date. KOSPI 200 futures track the KOSPI 200 index. prices suddenly swing, program-trading orders seeking to exploit the gap between futures and cash-market prices may flood into stocks (see Part 2).

A sidecar curb temporarily suspends incoming program orders on the affected side for five minutes. A sell-side sidecar curbs program sell orders during a sharp decline; a buy-side sidecar curbs program buy orders during a sharp rise. It does not stop ordinary stock trading.

For the KOSPI market, a sidecar is triggered when KOSPI 200 futures remain at least 5% above or below their reference price (usually the previous close) for one minute. The KOSDAQ has two simultaneous tests: KOSDAQ 150 futures must move at least 6%, and the KOSDAQ 150 cash index at least 3%, in the same direction for one minute. Each market allows just one sidecar a day—buy-side and sell-side combined—and none can be triggered during the first five minutes after the open.

Circuit breakers have three levels

The term comes from an electrical safety switch that cuts the current when it becomes excessive. Like a breaker in a home's fuse box, this safeguard briefly shuts down an entire market. When triggered on the KOSPI, it also halts related futures and options trading. Each level is activated when the KOSPI remains sufficiently below the previous close for one minute.

When it triggersWhat follows Level 1Down 8% or more20-min halt → reopening Level 2Down 15% or more; further 1% decline beyond Level 1 trigger20-min halt → reopening Level 3Down 20% or more; further 1% decline beyond Level 2 triggerTrading ends for the day; existing orders cannot be canceled

During the 20-minute halt, the exchange accepts no new orders, although existing orders may be canceled. Over the next 10 minutes, orders are collected and matched at a single price, as they are during VI. That is called a single-price call auctionAn auction that collects orders during a set interval and executes matching orders together at the price that allows the greatest trading volume..

Each circuit-breaker level can be triggered only once per trading day. Sidecars and Levels 1–2 cannot be activated during the final 40 minutes of the regular session—after 2:50 p.m.—but Level 3 remains possible. As of the end of July, Levels 2 and 3 had never been triggered.

03APPLY IT & RECAP

How to Read
Trading-Halt News

Does a circuit breaker stop prices from falling? Let's follow the KOSPI through November 6.

November 6: KOSPIA market that stopped—and reopened

  1. 09:03

    The index was near 4,920, about 8.5% below the previous close, when the Level 1 circuit breaker fired. The KOSPI had not fallen below 5,000 intraday since February 6—nine months earlier.

  2. 09:23

    The 20-minute trading halt ended, and the 10-minute single-price call auction began.

  3. 09:33

    The call auction ended, and continuous trading resumed. The 9:23 and 9:33 times are calculated from the prescribed 20- and 10-minute intervals.

  4. 09:34

    A sell-side sidecar curb was triggered. Sidecars cannot activate until five minutes after the open, which is why the circuit breaker came first that day.

  5. 10:33

    4,994.78. Even after reopening, the KOSPI was trading below 5,000.

  6. Close

    Down 6.02% at 5,059.69.

The index was down roughly 8.5% when the breaker triggered but finished the day down 6.02%. Circuit breakers respond to an intraday decline and how long it persists—not the eventual closing return.The intraday trigger and the close can tell very different stories.

On July 28, the opposite happened: a circuit breaker was triggered, yet the KOSPI still closed down 10.84%. Once trading resumes, prices can recover—or fall further.Halting trading is not the same as stopping a selloff.

Frequent trading curbs also signal an increasingly volatile market. In late July, Lee Jong-hyung, head of research at Kiwoom Securities, argued that repeated circuit breakers and sidecars can condition investors to expect panic, potentially amplifying volatility further.

Four questions to ask when trading stops

01Which safeguard?

First ask whether it was a sidecar or circuit breaker. The scope of the halt is different.

02Which market?

On November 5, the two markets saw sidecars more than four hours apart. Always check which market the report is about.

03What time?

Sidecars and Levels 1–2 cannot be triggered after 2:50 p.m. No trigger does not mean the decline was smaller.

04How did it close?

A trigger is an intraday event. Look at the closing report to understand how the full session ended.
COMMON MISCONCEPTIONSPlausible—but wrong
MYTH 01
"A sidecar stops all stock trading."
Only program orders on the affected side are curbed. Regular stock orders can still execute.
MYTH 02
"Circuit breakers also activate on sharp rallies."
KOSPI circuit breakers operate only on declines. A sharp rise can trigger a buy-side sidecar instead.
MYTH 03
"A stock's loss is capped at 30% once it hits its daily lower limit."
The ±30% band resets each trading day from the latest closing price. Two consecutive days at the lower limit mean a cumulative loss of more than 50%.
THE ONE SENTENCE TO REMEMBER · PART 17

A circuit breaker is the broadest market brake, bringing trading across an entire market to a stop.

But its purpose is to give investors time to reassess, not to end a selloff. Its activation alone tells you nothing about whether the market has bottomed.

THE TERMS, IN BRIEF
Circuit breaker
A marketwide trading halt triggered by a steep index decline. Applied separately to the KOSPI and KOSDAQ, with three escalating levels.
Sidecar trading curb
A five-minute suspension of program-trading orders on the relevant side after a sharp futures move.
Buy-side / sell-side sidecar
Buy-side curbs follow sharp rises; sell-side curbs follow sharp falls.
Program trading
Computer-generated orders to buy or sell baskets of stocks according to preset rules.
Futures contract
A contract to buy or sell an underlying asset at an agreed price on a specified future date.
Daily price-limit band
The permitted price range for most shares in a Korean trading session: generally ±30% of the prior close.
Daily upper / lower price limit
The uppermost and lowermost permitted prices. Trading does not automatically stop when a limit is reached.
VI
A Volatility Interruption (VI) moves one stock into a two-minute single-price call auction after an abrupt price move.
Single-price call auction
Orders are collected over a defined period and matched together at one price when the auction concludes.
Trading halt / suspension
At Levels 1–2, new orders are prohibited during the halt but cancellations are permitted. At Level 3, trading ends for the day and cancellations are barred too.
Previous closing price
The last price from the preceding trading session, commonly used to determine price limits and circuit-breaker thresholds.
REAL-WORLD CASES
July 28, 2026 · Closed down 10.84% despite a circuit breaker

Level 1 circuit breakers were triggered in both the KOSPI and KOSDAQ markets. The KOSPI finished down 10.84% at 6,023.66; the KOSDAQ lost 7.72% to close at 705.85. Sidecars were triggered 23 times during July.

March 4, 2026 · First circuit breakers of the year

As the U.S.–Iran conflict intensified, circuit breakers were triggered in both markets. It was the first simultaneous activation since August 2024. At 11:20 a.m., the KOSPI was down 8.10% and the KOSDAQ 8.13%.

June 15, 2015 · Price limits widened from ±15% to ±30%

The permitted daily move doubled, establishing the current standard upper and lower price limits.

HOW TO READ THE NUMBERS

11th this year vs. 17th on record· These counts refer only to the KOSPI; the KOSDAQ is tallied separately. Media outlets have reported different totals depending on their counting methods. This guide uses the Asia Business Daily's "11th in 2026, 17th on record" count alongside a list of six triggers during 2000–2025. No further circuit breaker was activated by the November 13 close.

Trigger times and index levels· Around 9:03 a.m. and the 4,920 area (roughly −8.5%) are figures reported in the news (YTN cited the 4,923 area and −8.55%). The 9:23 and 9:33 times are calculated from the required 20-minute halt and subsequent 10-minute call auction. Times around 9:34 a.m., 10:16 a.m. and 2:27 p.m. are approximate, as reported.

No Level 2 or Level 3 triggers· Based on reporting as of July 28 by the Herald Business. Of the later circuit breakers discussed here, only the November 6 event has a separately verified level (Level 1).

Two consecutive lower-limit sessions· 70% × 70% = 49% of the initial price remains, a loss of approximately 51%. This is a simplified calculation for illustration.
