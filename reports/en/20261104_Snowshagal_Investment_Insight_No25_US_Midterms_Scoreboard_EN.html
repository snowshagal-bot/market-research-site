Investment Insight · Midterm Scoreboard

In the November 3 U.S. midterms, Democrats flipped seven Republican-held Senate seats and more than 20 House seats. What changed most was the power to block legislation, not the power to enact it. For investors in South Korea, the watchlist extends beyond tariffs to U.S. Treasury yields, the won-dollar exchange rate, and actual orders received by companies in each sector.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

  1. The Democrats won control of Congress.They flipped seven Republican-held Senate seats and more than 20 House seats (AP and media tallies).1, 2 Final seat counts are still being tallied.
  2. The biggest change is blocking power.Overriding a veto requires 290 votes in the House and 67 in the Senate, assuming all seats are filled. From January 3, 2027, additional tax cuts and fiscal expansion will be much harder to enact.
  3. The president's tariff authority remains intact.Current tariffs rest on Section 301 of the Trade Act and Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act—authorities that do not require a new congressional vote. The legal powers remain; tariff rates can still change.
  4. The interest-rate and currency effects remain to be seen.In four of five previous shifts to divided government, the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield was lower six months later. That is a very small sample. On election day in Asia, USD/KRW moved just KRW 0.5.
  5. Most sector signals are still pending.The relevant checkpoints differ: state-level AI rules, the National Defense Authorization Act for shipbuilding, and specific tax-credit provisions for batteries.
00DAY ONE

The KOSPI Swung 400 Points Intraday.
The Won Moved Just KRW 0.5.

On November 4, the KOSPI opened more than 2% higher, fell more than 4% at one point, and closed up 0.62% at 5,683.80. The intraday high-low range was 406 points. Foreign investors sold a net roughly KRW 2.5 trillion in Korean equities.3 This unfolded as U.S. election results were being counted.

But the election was not the only driver that day. Semiconductor earnings, oil prices, and foreign investor flows were also in play.

OTHER FORCES AT WORK THAT DAY

  • AMD Q3 earnings (after the U.S. close on Nov. 3). Revenue of $13.38bn (+45% YoY), data-center revenue of $8.55bn (+97%), and Q4 revenue guidance of about $17bn.6 Reuters cited the results as one reason Korean stocks rose early in the November 4 Asian session.7
  • Apple −1.90% (Nov. 3). Its holiday-quarter margin outlook and warning about higher memory costs weighed on the shares.4
  • Oil prices. WTI crude closed November 2 at $96.46, down 7.36%, on hopes for a U.S.–Iran peace deal (Kiwoom Securities).8
  • How to interpret the intraday selloff. Kiwoom Securities pointed to concerns about data-center regulation. Yuanta Securities argued investors should distinguish construction delays from actual order cancellations.9
  • Prediction markets. Polymarket had put the probability of Democrats controlling both chambers at 66.5% before the election (Oct. 24; Daishin Securities).10 Anticipating victory and knowing the eventual margin are not the same thing.

Regulatory worries and booming AI-infrastructure demand coexisted that day. It would be misleading to attribute the entire session to any single factor.

WHY DID USD/KRW MOVE ONLY 0.5 WON?

  • The dollar weakened. The dollar index was down 0.55% in Asian trading on November 4.7 Even so, USD/KRW edged up by KRW 0.5.
  • Foreign investors were sellers. They sold a net roughly KRW 2.5tn on the KOSPI. How much that selling actually moved the exchange rate requires separate analysis.
  • One-day moves and longer-term transmission channels are different. Yields, the won, foreign flows, and sector orders should be tracked over months, not hours.

Two questions matter to readers: “If Democrats control both chambers, do tariffs go away?” “What will reach Korean stocks and the won—and when?” The answers start with who holds which legal powers, not with a partisan verdict.

02THE POWER MAP

Congress Holds the Spending and Tax Levers.
The President Still Holds the Tariff Lever.

The size of an electoral victory and the ability to carry out policy are two different things. Measures that require Congress face new constraints; powers the president can exercise under existing law remain available.

THE LEGAL BASIS FOR TARIFFS IS THE STARTING POINT

  1. 2026.02.20IEEPA reciprocal tariffs struck down by the Supreme CourtIn a 6–3 ruling, the Supreme Court found tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act unlawful.25
  2. 2026.02.24 ~ 07.24Temporary global tariffs under Section 122 expired after 150 daysExtending them required an act of Congress. They expired without an extension even under a Republican-controlled Congress.26
  3. 2026.07.24 ~Replaced by Section 301 tariffsThe stated basis is an investigation into enforcement of forced-labor import prohibitions. For Korean goods, the additional duty is calibrated to bring the combined tariff rate to 12.5%—not to add 12.5 percentage points. If the existing baseline tariff is already 12.5% or higher, the incremental duty is 0%; goods subject to Section 232 are excluded.27, 28, 29
  4. IN EFFECTProduct-specific Section 232 tariffsAutos: 15%; steel and aluminum: 50%; certain advanced-computing chips: 25% (January proclamation, with exemptions for uses including U.S. data centers); pharmaceuticals from countries with agreements: 15%.10, 30
  • Tariff-making powers remain with the president under the new Congress. The current tariffs rely on statutory authorities that do not require fresh congressional approval.
  • Unchanged authority does not mean frozen tariff rates. Further Section 301 investigations into overcapacity, the Jan. 10, 2027 expiration of the U.S.–China trade truce, and country-specific negotiations could all change rates.23, 31
  • Congress can still apply pressure. Repealing tariffs through legislation would have to overcome a presidential veto. Daishin Securities sees hearings and resolutions as more likely to slow escalation than to undo tariffs outright.10

Tariffs also feed into the fiscal outlook. The CBO projected that the disappearance of IEEPA tariffs and other changes would raise the cumulative deficit for 2027–2036 by about $900bn relative to its February forecast.32 Blocking additional tax cuts does not, by itself, mean the federal deficit will shrink.

WHAT THE HISTORY DOES—AND DOES NOT—SAY

  • The yield was lower six months later in four of five cases. The median move of −62bp matches Hana Securities' figure; the arithmetic mean was −40bp.
  • 2010 went the other way. Yields rose three and six months after that election (+89bp and +68bp). The Federal Reserve announced another $600bn in Treasury purchases (QE2) the day after the vote.34
  • These are five historical observations—not a forecast. They do not establish the probability or magnitude of a rate move in 2026. Hana Securities also cautioned that the sample is small and the current rate cycle differs.33

THE CASE FOR A WEAKER DOLLAR

Lower expectations for fiscal stimulus could weigh on the dollar (Saxo). A Democratic Congress constraining administration policy could also be dollar-negative (iM Securities).24, 35

THE CASE FOR A STRONGER DOLLAR

Stronger perceived Fed independence could support the dollar (Monex). Daishin Securities also views Senate confirmation power as a check affecting the Fed.10, 24

HOW THIS REACHES SOUTH KOREA

  • Lower U.S. long-term yields could reduce Korean government-bond yields and equity discount rates. The won also depends on expected U.S.–Korean policy-rate differentials and foreign flows. Next checkpoint: the Nov. 26 Bank of Korea meeting.
  • A stronger won can pressure exporters' profits. Hana Securities estimates that a KRW 10 move in USD/KRW changes annual operating profit at Korean automakers by roughly KRW 400bn–500bn.23 The impact varies considerably with local production and FX hedging.
  • On day one, “lower yields = stronger won = higher stocks” did not hold. USD/KRW was 1,341.5 (+0.5 won) and foreign investors sold a net KRW 2.5tn.3

11DEFENSE & SHIPBUILDINGPENDING

Foreign construction and U.S.-based construction are two separate paths

Defense spending
DS Securities argues that “Democratic Congress = lower defense spending” is a mistaken assumption. Defense outlays rose during past periods when Democrats controlled both chambers ($521.8bn in 2006 to $693.5bn in 2010; $724.6bn in 2020 to $765.6bn in 2022).44 These are nominal figures that include wartime spending and inflation. They do not show that the composition of Congress caused defense spending to increase.
Path 1 · Building overseas
  • Existing law: U.S. Navy vessels generally may not be built in foreign shipyards. The president may authorize an exception, with contracts possible 30 days after notifying Congress (10 U.S.C. §8679).45
  • Presidential memorandum (Aug. 13): The administration opened a route to build the first two ships in as many as three classes overseas—surface combatants, CONSOL tankers, and roll-on/roll-off vessels. Foreign builders must also establish a U.S. shipyard or acquire ownership or a majority stake in an existing one, train U.S. workers, transfer technology, and develop a U.S. supply chain. Each contract still requires a case-specific exception determination and congressional notice.46
  • Congress: The House proposal would bar Navy funding for foreign-built combatants. The Senate proposal would remove the president's exception authority but allow the first two auxiliary vessels to be built overseas. No final compromise bill has been agreed (SK Securities).47
Whether contracts materialize depends on the final law, appropriations, and Navy procurement decisions.
Path 2 · Building in the United States
Hanwha's Philly Shipyard won an order from TOTE to build one U.S. Navy landing ship medium (LSM), worth $269mn, with options for three more.48, 49 This is a domestic U.S. shipbuilding order, not a precedent for building U.S. Navy ships at yards in South Korea.
Research disagreement
Daishin Securities' regression found that HD Hyundai Heavy Industries tended to underperform as the probability of a Democratic sweep rose (a 10-percentage-point increase in probability was associated with −7.8 percentage points in relative performance, the only statistically significant Korean stock in the sample).10

06WHAT COULD CHANGE THE OUTLOOK

Three Variables That Could Delay
or Reverse the Thesis

Final seat counts and size of the victory

A larger Democratic House majority would give it more leverage in budget and debt-ceiling talks from January onward. Even so, Democrats would remain well short of 290 House and 67 Senate votes. Pepperstone argues a narrow victory may already have been largely priced in.7

Changes to the legal basis for tariffs

New Section 301 investigations or a different tariff instrument could change the assessment. Any measure requiring congressional approval would qualify the “unchanged” label. The U.S.–China trade truce also expires January 10.

Oil, Iran and AI earnings

Daishin Securities warns of oil-price and yield volatility if U.S.–Iran tensions escalate after the election.52 Nvidia's earnings could move semiconductor stocks more than the election itself.

Any of these variables could move yields and chip stocks more than the change in Congress. That is why the dashboard tracks verification dates for each issue rather than drawing a sweeping conclusion from the vote.

Watch More Than Tariff Rates:
Yields, the Won—and Actual Orders

The U.S. midterms constrain the administration's ability to pass additional legislation, but they do not repeal existing tariffs. After the new Congress convenes on January 3, 2027, additional tax cuts and fiscal expansion become more difficult, while the president retains existing tariff powers. For Korean equities, the path ahead will depend less on who won the election than on U.S. Treasury yields, the won, foreign investor flows, and changes in actual orders and corporate earnings.

  1. Why is the U.S. 10-year yield moving?After the Nov. 6 jobs report and Nov. 10 CPI, does any decline continue—and is it driven by fading fiscal-stimulus expectations or a deteriorating economy? The equity implications are very different.
  2. How much would a stronger won hurt exporters' earnings?Track how quickly USD/KRW approaches brokers' base cases in the low-1,300s, alongside the direction of foreign equity flows. For automakers, local production and FX hedging create company-specific exposures.
  3. Are sector orders and earnings following through?HBM orders, ESS contracts, U.S. Navy procurement, and CDMO wins will show whether policy expectations translate into revenue. December's funding and NDAA decisions belong to the current Congress; the new majorities take power on January 3. Use the calendar, not just the election headline.

Methodology, source differences and limits of interpretation
  • Senate map: Drawn by reconciling the AP tally (54 Democratic-caucus seats, 44 Republican seats, two uncalled; quoted by Yahoo News at 08:51 UTC on Nov. 4) with state-level reporting. The 54 Democratic-caucus seats consist of 34 not up for election (including two independents), 13 Democratic holds and seven flips.11
  • Mississippi race margin: The reported gap changed as counting progressed: 28 votes at 12:05 a.m. CT on Nov. 4, then 43 at 3 a.m. The AP tally could change again after publication.13
  • House seat flips: “More than 20” is a conservative formulation based on overnight media reports of 25 districts. Redistricting can lead outlets to define a “flip” differently. Broadcasters' projections of 244–245 Democratic seats are not an officially confirmed minimum.2, 9, 18
  • Governor races: The four R→D governorship flips follow NBC's tally at 06:11 UTC on Nov. 4. Arizona, Nevada, Kansas and Michigan were still uncalled at that time.16
  • Section 122 tariff rate: Brokerage reports cited 10% (Daishin and Hana Securities), while other reports referred to a 15% increase. The main text therefore does not specify a rate.
  • U.S. futures and Treasury yields in Asian trading on Nov. 4: Reuters' article text quoted S&P 500 futures at −0.5% and Nasdaq futures at −0.9%, while a live-price panel on the same page showed −1.08% and −2.01%, respectively. The snapshots are from different times. Likewise, the panel showed the 10-year yield at 4.952% (+5.8bp), whereas some news accounts described yields as falling. The main text uses only completed regular-session closes.7, 24
  • U.S. regular session on Nov. 3: The market closed before major results were called. It is a baseline, not a reaction to the election outcome.
  • AMD: After-hours share-price directions differed across reports, so they were omitted.
  • Foreign net selling on the KOSPI: Media estimates varied slightly: KRW 2.5084tn (Digital Daily) and KRW 2.5070tn (Business Watch).3, 9
  • Hana Securities' dollar and yield comparisons: Five transitions from unified to divided government among 16 elections since 1962, based on medians. The 2026 result marks the sixth transition.
  • DS Securities' defense-spending series: The report did not specify whether figures were nominal or exactly which accounting convention was used.
  • iM Securities (Nov. 3): The text refers to “21 instances since 1940,” although its table begins in 1942.
  • Brokerage reports: Public URLs were unavailable; citations therefore include report title, date, page numbers and chart identifiers. The Daishin regression, Hana dollar comparisons and IBK scenarios are taken from the original PDFs.
  • Limits of interpretation: Historical observations cannot explain yield or equity direction solely from congressional control (see No. 08, “The Rate Calendar Matters More Than the Election Calendar”). Sample sizes and medians are provided to make that limitation clear.
GLOSSARY
Divided government
The president's party does not control at least one chamber of Congress. The opposite is unified government.
Veto override
Congress repasses a bill the president has vetoed, with a two-thirds vote in each chamber.
Cloture / filibuster
Under the Senate's general rule, ending debate on most legislation requires 60 votes.
Budget reconciliation
A special process for certain tax and spending legislation that allows passage in the Senate by a simple majority (generally 51 votes). The 2025 tax law known as the OBBBA used reconciliation.
Lame-duck session
A session held by the outgoing Congress between the election and the swearing-in of the new Congress, generally in November and December.
National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA)
An annual law authorizing defense policy and spending levels. The House and Senate reconcile their versions into a final bill, often toward year-end.
Debt ceiling / X-date
The statutory limit on federal borrowing. The X-date is when the Treasury runs out of extraordinary measures and can no longer meet all obligations on time.
IEEPA
The International Emergency Economic Powers Act. It was used as the basis for the 2025 reciprocal tariffs, which the Supreme Court ruled unlawful in February 2026.
Section 122, Trade Act of 1974
Temporary import duties intended to address balance-of-payments problems; limited to 150 days unless Congress legislates an extension.
Section 301, Trade Act of 1974
Allows the executive branch to impose trade remedies following USTR investigations into unfair trade practices.
Section 232, Trade Expansion Act of 1962
National-security-based, product-specific tariffs, including on automobiles, steel, some semiconductors and pharmaceuticals.
45Y·48E·45X
U.S. tax credits: Section 45Y for clean-electricity production, Section 48E for qualifying clean-electricity and storage investment, and Section 45X for advanced manufacturing, including batteries.
Ranked-choice voting
Voters rank candidates. If none wins a majority, votes from the lowest-ranked candidate are redistributed according to voters' next preferences and the count continues.
