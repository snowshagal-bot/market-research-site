As the KOSPI approached a 50% drawdown, five brokerages published downside estimates on the same day. Their methods differed, but their numbers clustered between 4,050 and 4,600. Most drew on overlapping inputs: the same June peak, earnings forecasts and earlier crises. This is not a floor independently confirmed five times. It is a range that holds only if those assumptions do.

SUMMARYFive key takeaways
  1. The KOSPI has nearly halved since its June peak. On November 23, it fell to 4,586.80 intraday, triggering a sell-side trading curb for the second consecutive session. The 4,708.54 close was 48.3% below the June 22 closing high.
  2. Five brokerages put the downside between 4,050 and 4,600. Their yardsticks included forward and trailing price-to-book ratios, historical drawdowns and probability-weighted scenarios.
  3. The estimates converge partly because their starting assumptions overlap. Kiwoom, iM and Daishin applied P/B multiples to forecast-based book value. Yuanta and Daishin measured past crisis drawdowns against the same June peak. Only Hana relied on reported book value.
  4. There are two ways the estimates can fail. Earnings forecasts could be cut sharply, or valuations could fall below historical trough multiples even if earnings hold up.
  5. Earnings revisions are the first signal to watch. Forward P/E is already at its lowest since 2001, yet earnings forecasts have not turned down. Next come foreign selling, AI credit concerns and any deployment of the market-stabilization fund.

On Friday, November 20, the KOSPI closed down 5.21% at 4,869.96. That evening in the US, Reuters reported that the SEC was examining Nvidia's accounting, adding to the selloff that left Nvidia down 14%.1, 2 On Sunday, the government convened an emergency market-monitoring meeting and began preparations to activate the stock market stabilization fund, designed to support shares during a sharp decline.3

On Monday, November 23, the KOSPI opened down 2.77%. At 11:33 a.m., a sell-side program-trading sidecar was triggered for a second consecutive session.4 At 11:43 a.m., the index fell to 4,586.80 (−5.81%), before trimming its losses to close at 4,708.54 (−3.31%). The session low was just 30 points above 4,557—half of the June 22 closing peak of 9,114.55.

How much further could it fall? Five brokerages—Kiwoom, iM, Hana, Yuanta and Daishin—offered estimates that day. Their approaches differed, but the results clustered between 4,050 and 4,600. Understanding why they converge also reveals when they could be wrong.

01Depth and Speed

Fifth-deepest drawdown on record.
Fastest descent.

The November 23 close was 48.3% below the June 22 peak. Only the Asian financial crisis, the dot-com bust, the global financial crisis and the slump after South Korea's “three lows” boom were deeper. The index took just 4.5 months to fall 40%, the fastest of any historical bear market in Kiwoom's analysis.

Sources: Kiwoom Securities, FnGuide5 · Each selloff measured from the preceding peak (COVID episode: Jan. 2018 peak to Mar. 2020) · 2026 drawdown recalculated using the Nov. 23 close (4,708.54)

  • It was not just the large caps.Some 81% of KOSPI 200 constituents have fallen more than 40% from their highs—near the 85% seen during COVID and 88% in the global financial crisis.6
  • Earnings expectations are the key difference from earlier crises.The KOSPI's 12-month forward operating profit stands at a record KRW 1,415 trillion.7 We return to this in Section 03.
02Five Approaches

Five methods.
One broad range: 4,050–4,600.

First, two valuation yardsticks

P/E measures price relative to earnings; P/B compares price with book value (net assets). Dividing by expected figures over the next 12 months produces a *forward* multiple; using reported figures produces a *trailing* multiple. Forecast book value includes retained earnings expected to be added to today's book value. If profit forecasts fall, expected book value also falls, although not necessarily by the same percentage. Brokerage downside estimates generally apply valuation multiples observed in earlier crises to today's figures.

Kiwoom and iM use forward P/B, Hana uses trailing P/B, Yuanta uses historical drawdowns, and Daishin assigns probabilities to scenarios combining forward P/B, past drawdowns and wave analysis. Despite different methods, all five downside estimates lie 2–14% below the November 23 close.

03Shared Assumptions

Why do the numbers converge?
Different models, overlapping inputs.

Several of the five firms relied on the same June peak, similar earnings forecasts and the same earlier crises. That makes it difficult to treat five similar results as five independent confirmations.

  • Similar forecast-based book valuesKiwoom, iM and Daishin multiplied 12-month forward book value (around 5,350–5,400 index points), derived from earnings consensus, by forward P/B multiples of 0.75–0.84×. A multiple near 0.8× produces a level around 4,300.
  • The same June peakYuanta and Daishin applied historical crisis drawdowns to the June peak. A 50–55% fall from that level means a market roughly halved.
  • The same crisesKiwoom, iM, Yuanta and Daishin all used the 2008 global financial crisis as a reference. Daishin also drew on the 1997–98 declines and the 2018 low. Hana alone used a non-crisis reference: August 2025 P/B, before semiconductor stocks came to the fore.

One common input is now at a record high: earnings forecasts.

Between June 22 and November 20, the KOSPI fell 46.6%, while 12-month forward EPS rose 35.7%, according to Daishin Securities.2 Forward P/E thus contracted by roughly 61%. The decline was driven by the multiple, not falling earnings estimates.

On reported earnings, the trailing P/E of 8.05× is already near the 2008 low of 7.46×. On book value, however, trailing P/B of 1.20× remains far above past crisis lows of 0.59–0.83×. The gap reflects today's higher return on equity (ROE, estimated as P/B ÷ P/E):ROE approximately 15%, versus 5–11% at previous troughs. That profitability must persist for the P/B-based downside range to hold.

Forward P/E looks lower still: about 3.3–3.4× (Kiwoom and Daishin), less than half the trailing 8.05× shown in the table. Analysts are projecting more than twice the profits already reported for the next 12 months. Kiwoom argues the market price appears to anticipate a cut of more than 50% to those estimates.5 Yet forecasts are still rising. Semiconductor earnings estimates climbed 15.1% in the month after third-quarter results.7

04What Could Go Wrong

Two ways the estimates could fail.
Watch earnings revisions first.

All five downside estimates rest on two pillars: historical troughs remain relevant, and current earnings forecasts do not collapse. The range could fail if profit forecasts are cut rapidly, or if valuation multiples sink below earlier crisis lows despite resilient earnings. Forward P/E is already near 3.3×—its lowest since 2001—while earnings forecasts have yet to turn down.2

1. The pace of earnings downgrades

iM Securities examined how far the KOSPI fell after historical earnings estimates reached their peaks. In periods of slow downgrades (2012–14 and 2018–19), the index fell another 4–17%; in faster downgrades (2002, 2008 and 2022), it fell another 17–41%.7 If earnings estimates were peaking now, the 2–14% distance to the five firms' lower levels would overlap the slow-revision range but fall short of most fast-revision outcomes.

Sources: iM Securities, other brokerages, Korea Exchange7 · A simple application of historical percentages to today's index is shown in the expandable “Five Firms' Calculations” section

  • This cycle starts from a different point.In earlier episodes, the KOSPI was down 12–24% by the time earnings estimates peaked. This time the index had already fallen more than 45% before estimates turned lower.7 If prices have already discounted substantial downgrades, the further decline could be smaller than historical comparisons imply.
  • The bar for earnings is unusually high.Since the November 2023 trough, earnings estimates have risen 459%—almost four times the previous record increase of 121% in 2012. iM warns that once estimates begin falling from such heights, their normalization could take considerable time.7
  • Market bottoms have preceded the end of earnings cuts.In 2008 and 2022, the index bottomed 3.7 months after earnings estimates peaked; by then those estimates were down 9.0% and 10.5%, respectively.7

2. What if multiples keep falling while earnings hold up?

  • That is already what happened since June.As Section 03 showed, earnings estimates rose while valuation multiples fell. Daishin sees this as a problem of confidence and discount rates, rather than earnings.2
  • Multiples can fall below a supposedly safe crisis threshold.During COVID in 2020, forward P/B reached 0.68×. Applied to today's forecast book value, that implies 3,673. Daishin lists this separately as a scenario for a financial-crisis-level shock.2
  • A full-blown credit squeeze has not yet emerged.Daishin characterizes the current US equity selloff as a monetary-tightening and valuation-reset episode (historical declines of 20–36%), not a recession or credit-crisis episode (45–86% declines). The high-yield spread is also at 3.47 percentage points, below levels associated with acute credit stress.2, 10

First, what is the market-stabilization fund?

Formally the Securities Market Stabilization Fund, it pools commitments from financial firms and securities-market institutions to buy equities when markets are under acute stress.

  • Who provides the money?The available framework dates to March 2020, with KRW 10.76tn pledged. Twenty-three financial companies, including Korea Development Bank and five major financial holding groups, joined four market institutions, including Korea Securities Finance Corporation.5, 13
  • What does it buy?Index-tracking products, such as those linked to the KOSPI 200, rather than individual shares. The aim is to avoid favoring particular companies.13
  • When is the money invested?Commitments are drawn when needed through capital calls. In 2020, the fund called KRW 3tn in its first round and began with KRW 1tn, but the market rebound meant the money went into short-term money-market funds (MMFs), not equities.14
  • Has it ever bought shares?Yes. It invested approximately KRW 400bn in 2003 and KRW 501.5bn in 2008.15 The 2020 fund did not buy equities, while the 2022 arrangement only renewed commitments.5, 15 Actual purchases now would be the first since 2008—18 years ago.16
  • How large is it?KRW 10tn is roughly 0.3% of the KOSPI's current KRW 3,800tn market capitalization, or around ten days of the heavy net foreign selling seen on Nov. 20 (about KRW 1.1tn). Kiwoom notes that no decision has been announced on expanding the fund to reflect the market's size.5

Measures under consideration

  • Market-stabilization fund: Activation preparations begin (Nov. 22)At an emergency meeting chaired by the deputy prime minister, officials agreed to prepare for immediate activation if needed. They said the fund's size would depend on market conditions; no deployment date was set.3, 16
  • Higher daily share-buyback limit (FSC decision, Nov. 23)From Nov. 24 to Feb. 23, 2027, listed companies may place daily buyback orders for the full number of shares they have disclosed. The ceiling based on distributable profits remains unchanged, so this does not increase the funds available for repurchases.17
  • Three months of heightened surveillance of illegal short selling; exemption from margin-collateral maintenance rulesAuthorities said they were not considering additional short-selling restrictions.16, 18 Separately, from Nov. 9 through Jan. 31, 2027, securities firms were exempted from the requirement to maintain the standard 140% collateral ratio for margin loans.19

Historically, the fund has often mattered first as a signal of government support rather than through its purchasing power. Some markets bottomed on announcements alone; in other episodes, equities rose more than 40% over the year following actual deployment (Kiwoom Securities).5 See the expandable “History of the Stabilization Fund” section below.

CONCLUSIONConclusion

The mid-4,000s
are a conditional floor.

The mid-4,000s are not a bottom confirmed five times. They are the range suggested by models built on overlapping assumptions. If earnings estimates are cut rapidly, or if multiples fall below historical troughs despite resilient earnings, the five estimates could move lower together.

Earnings revisions deserve the closest attention. Forward P/E is already at its lowest since 2001. Watch whether KOSPI 12-month forward operating profit (now KRW 1,415tn) and EPS begin falling, and if so, how many percent per month. The projected book value behind several downside calculations will move with those estimates.

This report does not recommend buying or selling securities or predict the market bottom. The downside estimates represent the respective brokerages' assessments. Reference dates and calculations appear in the expandable sections below.

Glossary
P/B
The price-to-book ratio: market price divided by book value per share (BVPS). At 1×, market capitalization equals accounting equity. Less volatile than earnings, book value is often used to assess downside risk.
Forward P/B
P/B based on expected book value 12 months ahead. It incorporates brokerage earnings forecasts, so the projected book-value base also falls if those forecasts are downgraded.
Trailing P/B
P/B based on reported book value, without future earnings assumptions. The Korea Exchange publishes index P/B on this basis.
Earnings estimates
Analyst consensus forecasts for companies' profits. Here the term refers to the KOSPI's 12-month forward operating profit and EPS.
Market-stabilization fund
The Securities Market Stabilization Fund, funded by financial firms and market institutions to purchase KOSPI 200-linked products during sharp market declines. It has not bought equities since 2008; see Section 05.
Sell-side trading sidecar
A Korean program-trading curb triggered when KOSPI 200 futures fall more than 5% for one minute. It pauses program sell orders for five minutes.
ROE
Return on equity: net income divided by book equity. Approximately equal to P/B divided by P/E when the measures use comparable bases.
P/E
Price-to-earnings ratio: share price divided by earnings per share (EPS). Shows how much investors pay for each unit of earnings.
BVPS
Book value per share: a company's accounting equity divided by shares outstanding.
Selling climax
A phase when panicked selling accelerates and reaches an extreme.
CDS
Credit default swap: the cost of protection against a borrower defaulting. Higher spreads imply greater perceived credit risk.
High-yield spread
The yield gap between lower-rated corporate debt and US Treasuries. It generally widens as credit concerns rise.
