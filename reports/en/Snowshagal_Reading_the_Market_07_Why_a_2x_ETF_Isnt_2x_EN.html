MAY 27, 2026 · REAL MARKETThe day a wave of 2× products listed at once

On May 27, 2026, eight asset managers simultaneously listed 16 leveraged and inverse ETFs tied to a single stock—either Samsung Electronics or SK hynix. Some target twice the stock’s daily move; others target twice the daily move in the opposite direction. In this article, we’ll call the daily +2× product 2×, and the daily −2× product −2×. It is the −2× inverse ETF that Korean traders often nickname “gopbus.”

02MECHANICS & TERMS

The word that matters: daily

How does a leveraged ETF create 2× exposure?

If an investor puts in 100, the fund can use futures, swaps, or similar contracts so that gains and losses behave as if 200 were exposed to the stock. That 200 is called exposureThe amount of market exposure driving gains and losses. If you invest 100 and the fund has 200 of exposure, a 1% move in the underlying produces roughly a 2% move before costs and tracking differences.. Then around each market close, the fund resets that exposure to twice its current net asset value. That daily reset is called rebalancingResetting exposure each day so the fund maintains its stated daily multiple..

ON THE BOARD · THE ONE WORD TO REMEMBER

daily return × 2 ≠ period return × 2

The 2× target resets every day. Yesterday’s gains enlarge today’s base; yesterday’s losses shrink it. So a multi-day result is determined not by the period’s total move alone, but by how much the underlying moved on each individual day.

Why the −2× inverse path can stray even farther

Run the warm-up through a −2× inverse ETF: 100 → 80 → 96. It ends at the same −4% as the +2× ETF. But the simple-multiple expectations were very different: −2% for +2×, and +2% for −2×.

In all three examples, the −2× path deviated from the simple multiple by roughly three times as much as the +2× path did. That is not a universal “three-times” rule. When both daily moves and the period’s overall change are small, the ratio tends to approach three; depending on the path, it can be smaller or larger. Big back-and-forth swings tend to push results toward larger losses, while a strong trend relative to volatility can push them toward larger gains. The −2× product is even more path-dependent.

Four questions to ask whenever you read a leveraged-ETF claim

01Is the claim about one day or many days?

The 2× target applies to daily. Over multiple days, stop assuming the period return will be exactly 2×.

02How volatile was the path?

Even with the same endpoint, large daily swings make it more likely that a leveraged path lagged simple multiplication.

03Percent or percentage points?

A cumulative return falling by a certain number of percentage points and a price falling by the same percentage are not the same move.

04Theoretical path or actual ETF price?

Because of fees, premiums/discounts, and tracking error, actual ETF performance can differ from the theoretical path. Even two products targeting the same 2× multiple can diverge.
COMMON MISTAKESClaims that sound right—but are not
MISTAKE 01
“If the stock rises 20%, a 2× ETF rises 40%.”
If that 20% move happens in one day, that is the target before fees and tracking differences. If the stock gains 20% over multiple days, the ETF can return more or less than 40%, depending on the path.
MISTAKE 02
“If I correctly call a decline, a −2× ETF must make money.”
SK hynix fell over the seven-week period, yet the theoretical −2× path lost 33.0%. Getting the direction right is not enough; the path matters too.
MISTAKE 03
“2× ETF crashes 48% in one day.”
On June 23, one report said the cumulative return of an SK hynix 2× ETF fell from 87.63% to 39.82%. What fell by 47.81 was the cumulative return, measured in percentage points: 47.81 pp. Expressed as a price starting from 100, the level would move from 187.63 to 139.82—a decline of about 25%. SK hynix itself fell 12.47% that day. The reported cumulative return included the first trading day’s gain, so it uses a different starting point from the chart in this article, which starts at the listing-day close.

PART 7 · IN ONE SENTENCE

A leveraged ETF targets daily 2×. Over multiple days, the outcome is determined by the sequence of daily returns along the way.

A strong trend relative to volatility can make the compounded result better than simple multiplication; heavy back-and-forth movement that ends near the starting point makes it worse. That is why +2× and −2× products can both lose money even when they reference the same stock.

One-line glossary
ETF
A fund that trades on an exchange like a stock.
Leveraged ETF
An ETF targeting a stated multiple—such as 2×—of an underlying asset’s daily return.
Inverse ETF
An ETF targeting the opposite of an underlying asset’s daily return.
Gopbus
Korean market slang for a −2× inverse ETF.
Single-stock leverage
A leveraged ETF tied to one stock rather than an index.
Daily return
The return over one trading day. A leveraged ETF’s stated multiple applies to this return.
Cumulative return
The return from the starting point to the present.
Compounding
Gains and losses change the capital base, so the next day’s return applies to a different amount.
volatility drag
When repeated up-and-down moves cause a leveraged product to lag a simple multiple. Often called volatility drag or negative compounding.
exposure
The amount of market exposure that drives gains and losses.
rebalancing
Resetting exposure each day to maintain the stated daily multiple.
net asset value
The per-share value of an ETF’s assets minus its liabilities. Abbreviated NAV.
Premium / discount
How far the market price is above or below NAV.
Tracking error
The difference between the target return and the realized return.
Expense ratio
Annual operating and distribution costs, accrued gradually from fund assets.
%p
The difference between percentages. A move from 87% to 40% is a decline of 47 percentage points, not 47%.
Minimum deposit requirement
The minimum amount that must be in an account before trading certain leveraged products in Korea.
Pre-trading education
Mandatory online education required before trading certain leveraged products in Korea.
Trading leveraged ETFs in Korea

Before trading domestic or overseas leveraged products, Korean investors must satisfy both the pre-trading education requirement and Minimum deposit requirement.

Single-stock leveraged ETFs · From July 31, 2026, the minimum deposit requirement rose from KRW 10 million to KRW 30 million in cash. Proceeds from stock sales count only after settlement (T+2) credits the cash to the account.

Overseas-listed leveraged ETFs and ETNs · From May 22, 2026, first-time investors need a KRW 10 million minimum deposit, and the required pre-trading education was extended to two hours.

Detailed rules continue to change. Check your broker’s current requirements before trading.

How the theoretical paths were calculated—and what we left out

Method · We multiplied each daily close-to-close return by +2 or −2 and compounded the sequence. We excluded fees, market-price/NAV gaps, tracking error, dividends (SK hynix ex-dividend May 28; Samsung Electronics ex-dividend June 29), and product-specific implementation differences.

Actual ETF cumulative returns · News reports used different starting points—listing reference prices versus listing-day closes—and different end dates, making like-for-like comparisons difficult. We therefore calculated a consistent theoretical path from closing prices.

Actual size of rebalancing orders · We did not find an official aggregate of the actual orders executed around the close. The article therefore uses only a hypothetical fund with net assets of 100.

Total losses of retail investors · Estimates varied by methodology across reports, so we did not use a figure.

How far the −2× path can diverge from simple multiplication · When both daily moves and the total period change are small, the deviation for a −2× product tends to be roughly three times that of the +2× product; the three examples in the article were also near that ratio. But the ratio is path-dependent: about 2.4× for ten straight +2% days, and about 3.3× for two ±20% round trips.

We do not fill gaps with numbers we cannot verify.
