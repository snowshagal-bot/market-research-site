Investment Insight ⁠· NO.30

In January 2028, the statutory ceiling on KEPCO's outstanding bonds reverts from five times paid-in capital plus reserves to twice that amount. Under current projections, the outstanding balance would exceed the new ceiling by KRW 19.2 trillion. Extending the special limit would buy time—but not settle who pays for the electricity grid.

AT A GLANCE

  1. In January 2028, KEPCO's statutory bond ceiling reverts from 5× to 2× paid-in capital plus reserves. Projected bonds outstanding would exceed the new limit by KRW 19.2 trillion (NABO).
  2. Lower industrial tariffs and a tighter bond ceiling are separate policy decisions. They converge on one issue: how KEPCO finances its operations and investment.
  3. Even accumulating profits will not be enough to meet the limit. Although reserves are projected to grow 58.7% between 2025 and 2029, the bond-to-capital-and-reserves ratio would still stand at 2.1× in 2029—above the 2× ceiling.
  4. There are three routes: amend the law, strengthen the capital base, or reduce bonds outstanding. Each shifts the burden to a different group.
  5. Extending the deadline buys time; it does not reduce debt. Watch the National Assembly, the final regional tariff design, and the pace of earnings recovery.

Introduction

At a November 4 roundtable during an electricity industry exhibition in Gwangju, KEPCO President and CEO Kim Dong-cheol said a modest adjustment toward cost-reflective electricity tariffs needed to be discussed with the government. He also stressed that raising industrial tariffs should be a last resort, and that exchanges with the government had so far been at the working level.1 At a National Assembly audit in October, he likewise said the utility needed to discuss at least a minimal tariff increase with the government.2

KEPCO buys electricity from generators and resells it to businesses and households. If the government's August proposal lowers industrial tariffs in selected regions, the utility could lose roughly KRW 2.8 trillion in annual sales revenue.3 Lower wholesale purchase costs could offset part of that loss, but the size of the offset remains undetermined.

KEPCO has another, separate problem. The temporary increase in its statutory bond issuance ceiling, introduced amid heavy losses in 2022, expires at the end of 2027. Unless the law changes, the original 2× limit returns in 2028.4

The proposed tariff cuts do not directly cause the bond limit to fall. The policies are progressing independently, but collide in KEPCO's funding position: incoming cash could shrink while the legal capacity to finance itself through bonds contracts. The National Assembly Budget Office (NABO) estimates that projected bonds outstanding would exceed the new limit by about KRW 19.2 trillion.5

This does not mean KEPCO must repay KRW 19.2 trillion overnight, or that an immediate default follows. It means that, absent a legal change, KEPCO would have to reduce outstanding bonds or increase its qualifying capital base by enough to comply.

01THE CEILING

January 2028:
the statutory ceiling drops from 5× to 2×

Without an amendment, the projected end-2027 bond balance would exceed the new limit by KRW 19.2 trillion.

NABO classifies this shortfall as an amount requiring repayment.5 Without a legislative amendment or stronger capital base, KEPCO would need to repay or otherwise reduce bonds outstanding; both new issuance and refinancing maturing bonds could face constraints. The law does not, however, automatically accelerate the maturity of existing bonds.

WHAT THE LAW SAYS · ARTICLE 16 OF THE KEPCO ACT
  • General ruleThe bond limit is twice the sum of paid-in capital and reserves.
  • Temporary ruleA 5× ceiling applies through December 31, 2027.
  • Emergency exceptionA 6× ceiling may be authorized by the relevant minister. This proviso also expires on December 31, 2027, under the Act's supplementary provisions.

From 2028, even the emergency exception disappears. Without another amendment, only the original 2× rule remains.4

POINTS TO NOTE

  • The problem is the falling ceiling, not a worsening bond ratio. bond-to-capital-and-reserves ratio was 4.5× in 20236 and fell to 3.0× in 2025; it is forecast to decline further to 2.7× in 2027. Yet the projected ratio remains 2.3× in 2028 and 2.1× in 2029—still above the 2× ceiling.5
  • Bonds outstanding totaled KRW 73.7 trillion as of October 2. That comprised KRW 59.9 trillion in domestic KEPCO bonds, KRW 7.2 trillion in electronic short-term bonds, and KRW 6.6 trillion in offshore bonds. Other liabilities, including bank loans, do not enter this calculation.6
  • The same constraint emerged in 2022. Large losses threatened to deplete reserves when recognized in the year-end accounts and shut KEPCO out of additional bond issuance the following year. The temporary 5× limit was enacted in December 2022.7 The cover chart's 3.4× ratio for 2022 reflects the position after those losses were recorded.6

NABO's estimates are based on KEPCO's 2026–2030 medium-term financial management plan. If actual profits undershoot the plan, reserves will grow more slowly and the excess over the ceiling will rise; stronger profits would have the opposite effect.

If the company is already on a trajectory above its ceiling, the additional headroom first reduces the excess. It does not mean KEPCO can immediately borrow another KRW 2 trillion.

Why aren't profits enough?

  • Interest and taxes reduce earnings and slow the build-up of reserves. KEPCO's consolidated liabilities stood at KRW 210.7 trillion at end-June, while first-half interest expense averaged about KRW 11.5 billion per day.8 Consolidated liabilities cover the group, including generation subsidiaries; the KRW 73.7 trillion bond balance used for the legal ceiling is measured at KEPCO itself.
  • Grid investment absorbs cash well before the related expense hits the income statement. Capital spending is recorded as an asset and then expensed over time through depreciation. KEPCO can therefore be profitable yet still need to issue bonds to finance investment. Planned transmission and distribution spending over 2026–2030 totals KRW 55.3 trillion.9

Same year, operating-profit forecasts differ by more than twofold

  • Higher fuel costs are the main pressure on earnings. Between July and October, monthly average Brent prices rose 29% and Asian LNG prices 50%,14 yet the fuel-cost adjustment rate has been held at its +KRW 5/kWh ceiling for 18 consecutive quarters.11
  • Third-quarter results are due on November 12. DS and Hana forecast Q3 operating profit of about KRW 2.95tn (−47.9% year on year) and KRW 3.08tn (−45.5%), respectively. Both expect a Q4 operating loss: roughly KRW 1.15tn at DS and KRW 550bn at Hana.13, 14, 15
  • The recovery case hinges on nuclear generation. DS projects 2028 operating profit of KRW 9.1tn, assuming four reactors return to service from Q2 2027 and Saeul Units 3 and 4 come online.13

These are pre-earnings-release estimates from securities firms. Consolidated operating profit is not the same as the reserves of the KEPCO legal entity used in the bond-ceiling calculation.

DS EQUITY-RAISING SCENARIO · HYPOTHETICAL, NOT AN ANNOUNCED CAPITAL INCREASE
  • No share issuance has been decided. DS Investment & Securities calculated a scenario using assumed issuance terms.
  • KEPCO's market capitalization was roughly KRW 19.2tn at the November 9 share price of KRW 29,900—about the same as the projected excess above the bond ceiling. If KEPCO issued new shares at that price, KRW 9.6tn would equal about 50% of its market value, while BPS would fall by about 22%.13
  • If the government and Korea Development Bank subscribed in proportion to their combined 51.1% stake, the public-sector contribution would total roughly KRW 4.9tn.13
  • The actual cash required would depend on how proceeds above the KRW 5,000 par value count toward the ceiling, and whether the proceeds are used to repay bonds. A drop in book value per share does not automatically translate into the same share-price decline or a realized investor loss.

POINTS TO NOTE

  • Option ① can raise the ceiling without an immediate, large equity injection. But an extension merely pushes the statutory shortfall into the future; it does not cut outstanding bonds or interest expense. Even on the current plan, the ratio is projected at 2.1× in 2029.5
  • KEPCO cannot resolve these questions on its own. Tariff decisions rest with the government; statutory borrowing capacity rests with the National Assembly. DS notes that KEPCO is responsible for owning and operating the grid, while key decisions are spread across the government, legislature and other authorities.13

ADDITIONAL CONTEXT

Power equipment: It is too simplistic to say that KEPCO's inability to borrow would halt grid investment across the sector. Recent orders have largely been driven by U.S. demand. Korea's share of the value of U.S. imports of large oil-immersed transformers rose from 10.5% in 2023 to 18.0% in 2025 (DS Investment & Securities).13

Valuation gap versus U.S. utilities: KEPCO's PBR is forecast at 0.37× for 2026, compared with 2.59× for NextEra Energy and 1.81× for Duke Energy (Bloomberg consensus as cited by DS).13 U.S. utilities generally recover approved investment through regulated tariffs. Differences in growth, interest-rate exposure and business mix mean the valuation gap cannot be attributed to regulation alone.

THREE VARIABLES THAT COULD CHANGE THE OUTCOME OR ITS TIMING

1Energy prices

If expectations for an end to the Iran war bring oil prices down, earnings could recover and reserves could provide more support for the bond ceiling. Higher winter LNG costs would do the opposite. But the EIA has actually raised its 2027 average oil-price forecast. KEPCO's purchase costs also depend on LNG and coal, exchange rates and delays in passing costs through to tariffs; crude alone is not enough to judge the outcome.

2Speed of legislative change

An early extension would push the 2028 constraint further out. The precedent is not entirely reassuring: an amendment in 2022 was rejected once at the National Assembly plenary session on December 8 and was passed only later that month.4, 7

3Funding the regional tariff reform

KEPCO's exposure will depend on how much of the KRW 2.8tn is offset by procurement savings and government funding. Generators are demanding a three-year delay in LMP.16

KNOWN ESTABLISHED FACTS

  • Both the temporary 5× ceiling and the emergency 6× exception expire on Dec 31, 2027.
  • Proposed 2027 budget: KRW 500bn in new equity support; KRW 350bn to take over existing welfare-discount costs.
  • Q4 tariff freeze; regional industrial tariff proposal implies a KRW 2.8tn annual bill reduction.

ESTIMATE FORECASTS AND ESTIMATES

  • End-2027 bonds outstanding of KRW 76.0tn and a KRW 19.2tn excess (NABO).
  • Reserves up 58.7% through 2029, but bond ratio still 2.1× (NABO).
  • 2027 operating profit of KRW 4.1–4.6tn versus consensus of KRW 10.3tn.

UNKNOWN STILL UNKNOWN

  • Whether and when the KEPCO Act will be amended.
  • How much of the KRW 2.8tn tariff-revenue impact KEPCO will ultimately bear.
  • How much electricity tariffs will change in 2027.

CONCLUSION

An extension would buy time, but not answer who pays for electricity supply and grid expansion. Even with the planned accumulation of earnings, KEPCO would not meet the 2× ceiling. Current tariff decisions do little to expand its funding headroom, while equity injections raise questions of scale and dilution. Investors therefore need to ask not only how quickly profit recovers—and becomes reserves and cash flow—but also how securely the chosen bond-ceiling solution finances future grid investment.

METHODOLOGY
  • 2× ceiling: KRW 56.8tn = projected end-2027 capital plus reserves of KRW 28.4tn × 2. Excess: KRW 19.2tn = 76.0 − 56.8. NABO's more precise figures: KRW 76.0489tn − KRW 56.8354tn = KRW 19.2135tn.
  • Required multiple: approximately 2.7× = 76.0 ÷ 28.4. At 3×, the limit would be about KRW 85.3tn.
  • Additional qualifying capital and reserves: KRW 9.6tn = 19.2 ÷ 2, assuming bonds outstanding stay constant. The cash needed if this is achieved through new equity depends on how paid-in amounts above par value qualify and whether bond debt is repaid.
  • Indicative effect of a KRW 500bn government equity contribution: around KRW 1tn of extra capacity = 0.5tn × 2, assuming the full contribution qualifies under the statutory formula.
  • DS equity scenario: shares issued at the Nov 9 close of KRW 29,900; roughly 50% of KEPCO's KRW 19.2tn market capitalization; about 320m new shares; consolidated BPS falling about 22%, from KRW 86,976 to roughly KRW 68,000; about KRW 4.9tn from the government and Korea Development Bank if they subscribe in line with their combined 51.1% holding.
  • KEPCO's net burden from regional tariffs = lost tariff revenue − actual electricity-purchase savings − fiscal compensation recognized in earnings. The last two components are not yet specified, so no net amount has been calculated.
  • Reserves arise from KEPCO's own statutory accounts, but do not move one-for-one with consolidated operating income because of interest, tax, dividends and the treatment of losses.
  • Growth in reserves of 58.7% = KRW 33.5252tn ÷ KRW 21.1207tn − 1 (NABO).
  • Bar lengths in the earnings-forecast chart are indexed to KRW 13.5tn = 100.

GLOSSARY
Statutory bond ceiling
The legal maximum amount of KEPCO bonds outstanding. Article 16 of the KEPCO Act defines it as a multiple of KEPCO's own paid-in capital and reserves. Bank borrowing and other liabilities are not included.
Paid-in capital and reserves
The denominator used to calculate the bond ceiling. Paid-in capital comes from shareholders; reserves reflect accumulated profits. Profits tend to build reserves, while losses may erode them.
Bond-to-capital-and-reserves ratio
Outstanding bonds divided by paid-in capital plus reserves. From 2028, the ratio may not exceed 2× unless the law is amended.
Locational marginal pricing (LMP)
A system that sets wholesale electricity prices by location. Under the Korean draft, prices in the Seoul metropolitan area would stay near current levels, while prices could fall in generation-heavy non-capital regions when transmission congestion limits the flow of electricity.
National Assembly Budget Office (NABO)
A fiscal analysis body of South Korea's National Assembly. Its projected ceiling shortfall is based on KEPCO's medium-term financial management plan.
Electronic short-term bonds
Debt securities issued in electronic form with maturities of one year or less. They are included in KEPCO's outstanding bond balance for this purpose.
Fuel-cost adjustment rate
The electricity-tariff component that reflects changes in fuel prices. It is set quarterly, subject to a range of plus or minus KRW 5 per kWh.
Government cash equity contribution
A cash injection from the government that increases KEPCO's paid-in capital. The statutory ceiling rises to the extent the contributed amount qualifies under the formula.
Cost-causation principle
The principle that the party creating new electricity demand—such as a factory or data center—contributes to the cost of building the required grid infrastructure.
BPS
Book value per share: a company's net assets divided by its shares outstanding. Issuing new equity below book value can reduce existing shareholders' book value per share (dilution).
Advance payment of electricity bills
A financing method in which customers pay several years of electricity bills up front, allowing KEPCO to secure cash without issuing new bonds.
Private-sector build–transfer (BT)
A model under which private developers build a transmission network and transfer it to KEPCO. It may defer borrowing during construction until after completion, not eliminate the cost or debt burden.
PBR
Price-to-book ratio: share price divided by book value per share. A ratio below 1× means the stock trades below accounting book value.
Public-enterprise bonds
Bonds issued by state-owned or public corporations, including KEPCO bonds.
