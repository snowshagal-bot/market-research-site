The most common explanation is simple: earnings missed market expectations. That part is true. Reported operating profit came in more than ₩3tn below consensusThe average of brokerage estimates. 02Earnings & the Price Tag

Earnings Can Rise
While the Price Falls

A stock price is a single number, but two gauges sit underneath it: the earnings the company produces, and the multiple the market assigns to those earnings.

The Simplest Way to Decompose a Stock Price

EPSEarnings per share. Net income divided by shares outstanding—the amount of net income attributable to each share.
Business performance and share count drive it
Operating performance, non-operating items, taxes, and shares outstanding all feed into it.
×
PERPrice-to-earnings ratio. Share price divided by EPS—the price the market pays for one unit of earnings.
Growth expectations, rates, and risk drive it
External conditions matter heavily. The multiple can change within a single day.
EPS · Earnings per Share × P/E · Multiple on Those Earnings = STOCK PRICE

This is an explanatory decomposition only when EPS and P/E use the same basis. It is not a formula for calculating fair value for every company.

Company results feed more directly into the left-hand gauge. The market sets the multiple on the right. So even if the left side rises, the stock can fall if the right side contracts by more. This episode does not try to decide what multiple is “fair.” Its question is what moves that multiple?

EPS increased, but the multiple investors were willing to pay fell even more, so the share price declined. This is one way a stock can fall even as earnings improve.

Which Earnings Are You Using?

PAST
Reported earnings
Already published. Trailing P/EP/E calculated using already reported recent earnings in the denominator. Also called trailing P/E. uses these earnings.
PRESENT
Current share price
Visible on the screen now. It is the numerator in both P/E measures.
FUTURE
Earnings forecast
Not yet realized. Forward P/EP/E calculated using forecast future earnings in the denominator. uses these earnings.
DO NOT MIX PERIODS. Multiplying EPS and P/E from different periods does not reproduce any meaningful share price. And if forecast earnings are revised down, forward P/E rises even if the stock price does not move.

The company publishes the first document. The market keeps rewriting the third. And the market is often more sensitive to how much the third document has changed than to the first document itself. The next case shows that disconnect unfolding within a single day.

Consensus Is Not One Fixed Number

One fact became clear while preparing this episode: consensus estimates for the same company and quarter differed across sources.

SK hynix Q2 operating-profit consensusValue Reports citing FnGuide₩63.9868tn “Market forecast” cited by other media₩63.5526tn Samsung Electronics Q2 operating-profit consensus₩84.1894tn · ₩85.0494tn

The reason is straightforward: which provider collected estimates from how many brokerages, using what cutoff date differs. Some brokerages revise estimates right up to the release.

So when you read “earnings missed consensus by 5%,” you also need to ask which consensus? A different benchmark can turn 5% into 4.7% or 5.4%. If the conclusion hinges on that narrow a range, the conclusion was never more precise than the underlying estimate.
What “Priced In” Means

priced inA future event or outcome that the market has already incorporated into today’s price. means an expected future outcome is already reflected in the current price. If strong earnings are widely expected, the stock may rise before the release, leaving only confirmation on the announcement day.

But “priced in” is also an easy explanation to attach after the fact: if the stock rises, expectations were strong; if it falls, the good news was already priced in. There is no precise way to measure in advance exactly how much has already been priced in.

The reverse can also happen. Earnings may fall while the market prices in a recovery and expands the multiple, lifting the stock. That is the earlier example run in reverse.

03Two Separate Scorecards

Fill Them Out Separately.
Do Not Add Them Together

Everything so far can be reduced to three statements.

①
This is a good company
Business model and competitive strength · Built by the company · changes slowly
②
This quarter’s results were good
Results of the past quarter · Reported by the company · changes each quarter
③
This stock is attractively priced today
Relationship between expectations and price · Set by the market · can change within a day

Statement ① can be true while ② is false. Even if both ① and ② are true, ③ is a separate question. The three companies on July 29 and 30 illustrate that distinction. So the analysis needs two separate scorecards.

COMPANY SCORECARD① ②
  • What does the company sell, and how does it make money?
  • How many quarters has that profit persisted?
  • What differentiates it from competitors?
  • What breaks first when earnings weaken?
DO NOT ADD
THE TWO SCORES
STOCK SCORECARD③
  • What is the market already expecting?
  • Did the release beat or miss those expectations?
  • Did the company’s guidanceManagement’s own outlook for future earnings or industry conditions. raise or lower expectations?
  • How much are investors paying for those expectations today?

The company scorecard can be completely filled out while the stock scorecard still has blanks. They answer different questions. Adding them together only blurs both answers. Use both, but do not let a strong company score substitute for unanswered questions about the stock.

YOU CAN SAY THIS MUCH
  • Whether the reported result beat or missed market expectations
  • What management said about the next quarter
  • Where the stock opened and closed that day
YOU CANNOT CONFIRM THIS WITHOUT MORE DATA
  • Attributing that day’s entire move to earnings alone
  • Calling the stock expensive or cheap from P/E alone
  • Assuming good earnings guarantee a subsequent rise
More Data Can Narrow the Range
Release time & intraday price actionExcess return vs. index and peer groupConsensus revisions & guidanceVolume & investor flowsPre-release stock performance

This still does not prove causality, but it can narrow which explanation is more plausible. “Expensive” or “cheap” has no absolute answer either, but it becomes analyzable once you define the comparison framework.

EPISODE 4 IN ONE SENTENCE
A stock price reflects more than the company’s current report card. It also reflects what the market already expected, the outlook for future earnings, and the price investors are willing to pay for those expectations.
The company writes the report card; the market writes the expectations and the price tag. They overlap in one stock price, so that single number cannot evaluate the company by itself.

News headlines often compress all three into one sentence. “The stock fell despite good earnings” combines ② and ③. The reader’s job is to separate them again. Once separated, the missing pieces become visible. Leave unverified boxes empty—and be explicit about what evidence would be needed to fill them.
