Introduction

These developments revive a familiar phrase: “AI circular financing.” Google and Amazon invest in Anthropic, which then spends that money buying cloud capacity from Google and Amazon. AMD has agreed to buy Anthropic shares as equipment powered by its chips is deployed. The money seems to come full circle, raising the suspicion that reported revenue is being inflated.

But circular flows alone do not prove a bubble. If genuine customers use the services and pay for them, there is a real business. Anthropic's revenue, in fact, grew 14.7-fold over the past year. The problem is the sequence of cash flows. Bills for chips, servers and data centers are committed to first; customer cash arrives later. Equity investment and borrowing bridge that interval. If collections fall behind, someone must absorb the loss.

This issue therefore looks beyond the question of whether the financing is “circular” to ask how long the money takes to come back, and who carries the risk while everyone waits. We follow the contracts and financial statements in Anthropic's IPO filing, distinguishing cash received from customers, money invested or lent to the company, and gains that exist only as accounting valuations. Short on time? The five points below tell the story.

There is no single explanation for these moves. Securing financing makes near-term server and memory orders more likely to materialize. At the same time, it increases the cost of keeping those facilities running and servicing leases and debt over many years. Equity markets react first to imminent orders; credit markets scrutinize the longer-term repayment risk. The difference is the timing gap.

Three Different Kinds of Money

The circular-financing debate gets muddled when fundamentally different flows are lumped together. Separating the money into three categories makes the issue clearer.

The danger is not that revenue appears at several points in the supply chain. Equity and loan proceeds are real money, and building servers with that capital to sell services naturally creates revenue for multiple suppliers. The danger lies in concentrating investment exposure, revenue and collateral with the same counterparty: when it falters, several assets can lose value at once. The IMF calls this “wrong-way risk”—the value of your equity and loans falls precisely when the revenue expected from that same business comes under pressure.5

Not Every AI Company Is Alike

Anthropic is our central case study. But AI companies differ in how they earn and borrow money. Model developers, cloud providers and chip and memory manufacturers have different cash-generation models, payback periods and debt structures. Amazon, for example, has AWS operating income ($16.6 billion in the second quarter) and operating cash flow from its core businesses, independently of any gains on Anthropic holdings.6 Anthropic's figures alone cannot establish the return on Meta's AI investment or Amazon's overall ability to repay its obligations.

Are Customers
Actually Paying?

To meet long-term contractual obligations, customer revenue must ultimately turn into cash receipts. Revenue is the fastest-changing number in the S-1.

Customer LineCompute Line

Revenue grew faster than compute costs. Compare the first quarter of 2025 with the second quarter of 2026:

  • Quarterly revenue $376 million → $11.56 billion, or 14.7 times the year-earlier figure of $790 million.
  • Compute and infrastructure costs $940 million → $5.56 billion. As a share of revenue, costs fell from 251% to 48%.
  • Revenue ÷ compute costs 0.40× → 2.08×4, 7

Second-quarter operating income was $970 million, its first quarterly operating profit. Operating cash flow for the first half turned positive at $3.9 billion, versus negative $2.6 billion for full-year 2025.

But working capital contributed to that cash-flow result. Accounts receivable increased roughly $5 billion, reducing cash. An approximately $2.7 billion rise in accounts payable and accrued expenses—and roughly $1.4 billion more in deferred revenue collected from customers—provided an offset.4

The pace at which usage becomes recognized revenue, revenue becomes collected cash, and the company pays for infrastructure must be measured separately.

What the 2.08× Ratio Does Not Tell Us

The ratio shows progress toward monetization. On its own, however, it cannot tell us:

  • Investment returns or debt-servicing capacity; those require different figures.
  • The payback period; training and inference have different recovery profiles.
  • The timing of collections; recognizing revenue is not receiving cash.
  • Cash available for repayment; payroll, operating expenses, capex, interest and principal all have to be covered. Even when revenue outpaces compute costs, losses can widen if other expenses grow faster.

Revenue is not concentrated among a handful of large customers, either. No single customer accounts for more than 10%; in the third quarter, roughly 79% of revenue came from outside the top five customers, up from 64% in the first quarter.4

“48% Sold Through Cloud Marketplaces” Does Not Mean Circular Revenue

iM Securities estimated that 47% of Anthropic's revenue (about 70% on another page of the same report) flowed through Amazon and Google.8 Here is what the S-1 actually establishes:

  • 48% of revenue Sales through the marketplaces of three cloud providers—Amazon, Google and Microsoft—in H1 2026. That channel grew from 11% in 2023 to 32% in 2024 and 47% in 2025.
  • 58% of receivables Amounts due through those same channels at June-end.
  • 70% Not verifiable from the figures in the S-1.4

In these sales, the cloud provider bills and collects from the end customer, deducts its fee, and remits the proceeds to Anthropic. Anthropic contracts directly with the customer. The end user is the purchaser; the cloud provider serves as the payment and distribution channel. The risk lies elsewhere: nearly half of revenue and more than half of receivables move through three companies that are also competitors.

Those cloud providers are also Anthropic customers. The S-1 says they buy Anthropic services for their own use or products, but does not disclose the amounts.

Third-quarter figures have not yet appeared in the S-1. According to Kiwoom Securities, citing Financial Times and Bloomberg reporting dated October 14, Q3 revenue reached $16.8 billion, compute costs accounted for 59% of revenue, and the adjusted operating margin was 11%. One interpretation of the decline from Q2 (48% and 19%, respectively) is that additional compute costs were recognized ahead of revenue.9 The quarterly results thus show the same timing gap. Those figures still need to be verified against an amended S-1 (Form S-1/A).

Cho Jun-gi of SK Securities, citing a Bank for International Settlements (BIS) report released October 1, noted that among investments between AI companies in 2021–2025, transactions where the investor and investee also had a supplier–customer relationship accounted for 16.1% of deals by count but 46.4% by value.10, 11 In other words, investments involving intertwined commercial relationships tended to be larger.

One Company, Five Different Roles

The S-1 describes this overlap in the company's own words. Amazon, Google and Microsoft hold substantial stakes in Anthropic, supply compute, sell its models through their cloud platforms and collect payments. They also buy Anthropic services for internal use or their own products—and develop rival models. Anthropic warns that some cloud and chip partners supply capital or credit support while serving as infrastructure providers and competitors, potentially creating conflicts of interest over both compute-access terms and financing terms.4

Where Does a Dollar Invested by Google Go?

Kim Jun-young of iM Securities argues that circular financing merely expands sales among interconnected businesses without creating new funding.8 Following one dollar invested by Google helps show what actually needs to be verified.

Seven Contracts with Disclosed Amounts in the S-1—and Two Items Not in the Table

The filing's compute agreements fall into three categories based on how payments are determined.

  • Four minimum-payment contracts Amounts owed regardless of usage: Microsoft (Azure), at least $44.9B; Google (TPUs), about $114.5B or more; Amazon (Trainium), at least $110B; and Broadcom (TPU equipment finance leases, excluding interest), roughly $161.2B. Google and Amazon require shortfall payments if Anthropic uses less than the committed capacity.
  • Three usage-based agreements Contracts with emerging GPU-cloud providers, or “neoclouds”: SpaceXAI, up to $120.5B; Nscale, up to about $44.6B; and Lambda, around $41.3B. These are ceilings or charges tied to delivered capacity. SpaceXAI can terminate on 90 days' notice after its noncancelable period expires in early 2027. Of Lambda's agreements, $34.9B is contingent on obtaining facility leases.
  • Two items outside the contract table Additional TPU capacity expected from suppliers including Google in 2028–2031 (estimated $125B–$150B), plus AMD-based Helios equipment. Because the TPU capacity is not allocated by supplier, it cannot be treated as a single Google contract. Helios equipment may be purchased from approved manufacturers or used through third-party service providers. Anthropic expects to spend more than $20B if its initial deployment is completed; AMD's direct share is not disclosed.4

The Widely Quoted “$637B” Is Not a Number in the S-1

The widely circulated $637 billion “commitment total” is the arithmetic sum of the seven contracts above. The S-1 does not provide that total. It leaves blank both the total-commitments field and the amount payable through 2030, saying only that most payments remaining after that point fall in 2031–2045. How much of the entire commitment falls after 2030 is unknown.4

Even among those seven deals, the $430.6 billion in noncancelable minimums and $206.4 billion in ceilings are not equivalent exposures. Kiwoom Securities analyst Park Gi-hyun also tabulated them separately.9

The financial-statement notes provide a different set of figures, showing the changes between year-end 2025 and June 30, 2026.

  • Noncancelable purchase commitments $54.6B → approximately $265.6B; 4.9×
  • Leases not yet commenced $50.2B → $321.4B; 6.4×. Some depend on contractual conditions being satisfied.
  • Combined $104.8B → $587B; 5.6×4

The $587 billion is a June 30 snapshot. Subsequent agreements appear separately in the S-1's subsequent-events note.

  • Approximately $245B Future minimum payments under new or amended lease and commercial agreements after June 30, largely for compute and infrastructure. They begin in 2026–2036 and can run as long as 20 years. Some become effective only when conditions are met.
  • Approximately $21B Separate noncancelable short-term agreements.4

Because amended agreements may overlap with earlier commitments, these amounts cannot simply be added to $587 billion. An amended S-1 (S-1/A) is needed to establish the total exposure and payments due over the next 12 months.

The Seller
Also Provides the Financing

Google, Amazon, Broadcom and AMD supply Anthropic with compute or equipment—and have supplied, or agreed to supply, capital as well. But most of the remaining funding consists of conditional facilities. The timing of those inflows does not necessarily match the timing of the payments owed under commercial contracts.

Investment LineCompute Line

Google and Amazon are both investors and Anthropic's principal compute suppliers. For each, potential future funding exceeds the amount already invested. Those facilities become available only if Anthropic meets revenue targets or expands its purchases of the provider's compute. With Amazon, more spending on Amazon compute unlocks more Amazon funding.4

Across these four companies, $30 billion already invested plus $82 billion in conditional facilities is equivalent to roughly 28% of the $405.7 billion in multi-year minimum contracts and expected AMD-based equipment spending. This is a comparison of scale, illustrating the overlap between suppliers and capital providers—not a financing shortfall calculation. To determine any shortfall, we must also know how investment proceeds were spent and when facilities can be drawn.

Four Contract Mechanisms Worth Understanding

Broadcom

Financing Gets More Expensive When Business Weakens

Broadcom leases TPU equipment under five-year finance leases (roughly $161.2B in lease payments, excluding interest) and has made available a $42B convertible-note facility to help fund the payments. The 9% annual interest is added to principal rather than paid in cash. If notes are issued while specified revenue conditions are unmet, however, the rate rises to 13%, and the conversion price falls from 90% to 85% of the 30-day average share price. If the conditions remain unmet for too long, issuance is barred altogether. Financing terms deteriorate precisely when the company may need cash most.

AMD

It Buys Shares as GPUs Are Deployed

In July, Anthropic signed a master supply agreement with AMD for Helios rack-scale hardware (MI450). The equipment may be acquired from approved manufacturers or accessed through third-party service providers. AMD agreed to purchase up to $5B of Anthropic Class N nonvoting stock. Funding becomes available in installments as GPU delivery and installation milestones are met, with the first milestone expected in 2027.

Amazon

Debt Can Be Offset Against Commercial Payments

Amazon's notes may convert into shares only as long as its equity stake does not exceed 12%. Amounts beyond that limit are reissued as interest-free rollover notes. Any outstanding balance at maturity may be repaid in cash or, at Amazon's option, set off against amounts Amazon owes Anthropic under their commercial agreements. The S-1 does not specify the amounts eligible for setoff.

Leases

Rent Can Be Paid in Convertible Notes

Anthropic may pay some rent owed by consolidated leasing subsidiaries through convertible notes rather than cash. It also says certain leases not yet commenced may be settled by issuing convertible notes. No such notes had actually been issued as of June 30.

The Pattern Appears at Meta Too: Announced Capital Is Not Collected Cash

Chipmakers buying shares of their chip customers are not unique to Anthropic. In Meta's October 26 equity offering announcement, Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom expressed interest in buying up to $5 billion of shares.14 After pricing, the common-stock transaction rose to $25 billion. Add $12.5 billion of mandatory convertible preferred stock and an at-the-market (ATM) issuance program of up to $60 billion beginning in early 2027, and the planned maximum reaches $97.5 billion.15, 14

Only a portion of that figure has actually reached Meta's bank account.

  • Cash already received About $12.5B of preferred stock was issued on October 29. It carries a 6.50% cumulative annual dividend—roughly $812.5 million per year—but the board may elect to pay in shares rather than cash, so the full amount need not be a cash outflow.
  • Cash not yet received The roughly $25B common-stock transaction is a forward sale, with settlements scheduled through November 19, 2027. The ATM program of up to $60B is an unissued authorization, not proceeds in hand.15, 16

Why Revenue, Profit
and Cash Are Different

In a circular-financing structure, the largest apparent windfall is often a valuation gain, not cash. When Anthropic's valuation rises, one company's books show a loss and another's show a gain.

Valuation LineInvestment Line

Anthropic's preferred-share issue price rose from $11.23 in its 2023 Series C to $589.01 in its May 2026 Series H (pre-split basis). It more than doubled—2.3×—in just four months from the January Series G price of $259.14.4

A higher valuation also increases the value of convertible notes issued by Anthropic: they confer the right to convert into equity at a much lower predetermined price. Here is the position at June-end.

  • Outstanding convertible notes All were Amazon-held interest-free notes with about $6.2B in principal
  • Conversion prices $20.93 and $50.48 a share (pre-split), or 3.6% and 8.6% of the Series H price
  • Fair value on the books $108.6B, or 17.5 times principal4, 9

A Loss on One Side, a Gain on the Other

This increase in value produces opposite accounting entries for the two companies.

  • Anthropic · Loss Second-quarter operating income turned positive for the first time at $970M. But a $59.6B fair-value loss on convertible notes and derivatives, plus $8.4B of other non-operating losses, brought the net loss to $66.3B. Of the $80.7B first-half net loss, $72.4B came from fair-value movements.4
  • Amazon · Gain Other non-operating pretax income was $53.4B in Q2—about 66% of $80.9B pretax income. Amazon said Anthropic was the main driver. The first-quarter figure was $16.8B.6 This is the figure iM Securities described as roughly two-thirds of Amazon's pretax income.8

Amazon's Q2 Form 10-Q shows that $50.5B of the $53.4B (about 95%) came from upward fair-value adjustments to investments in private companies, which it attributed primarily to changes in the value of Anthropic nonvoting preferred shares.17 Most of that other income was a noncash valuation adjustment.

A larger accounting gain does not mean an equal amount of cash arrived. Amazon's trailing-12-month free cash flow was negative $7.6B at June-end; it deteriorated to negative $42.4B at September-end as equipment purchases accelerated.6, 18

An IPO changes the accounting. Once the notes convert to equity, subsequent remeasurement gains and losses on those liabilities disappear. That is why IBK Investment & Securities and Hyundai Motor Securities expect most of the post-IPO mark-to-market losses to cease.13, 7 Previously recognized losses remain, however, and the conversion dilutes existing shareholders.

Unrealized gains are volatile. Amazon's third-quarter earnings, released October 29, illustrate that clearly.

  • Other non-operating income / loss: −$700M The $53.4B valuation gain seen in Q2 did not recur.
  • Operating income: $28.1B Up 61% year over year.
  • Net income: $20.7B It actually fell. The year-earlier quarter included a $9.5B pretax mark-to-market gain on the Anthropic investment.18

After listing, Amazon and Google would mark their holdings against Anthropic's publicly traded share price, potentially making quarterly earnings sensitive to fluctuations in that stock.

When Chip-Supply Relationships Turn into Financing Relationships

Broadcom's $63B Deal: The Scale of Equipment Finance Comes Into Focus

Broadcom's equipment financing structure emerged in two stages.

  • June 9 Broadcom established the “AI XPV Platform” with Apollo and Blackstone. An initial $35B transaction led by Apollo supports an expansion of more than 1 GW of Anthropic training and inference capacity.21
  • October 26 Bank of America, Blackstone, Citi and Morgan Stanley announced a $63B transaction and financing structure. The $63B refers to the overall transaction size; the loan principal was not separately disclosed.

A newly formed SPV uses the financiers' capital to acquire Broadcom AI accelerators and networking equipment and lease them to Anthropic. Bank of America, Citi and Morgan Stanley arrange the first-lien (senior) debt. Blackstone alone provides the committed second-lien (junior) tranche. Broadcom supplies residual-value support to the SPV in an amount equal to the first-lien debt. Deliveries begin in 2027.12

Residual-value support is a contingent safeguard, triggered only if things go wrong. If equipment depreciates more than expected or a lease ends early, it provides a route for some of that risk to return to the equipment supplier. The BIS likewise highlights structures where chipmakers combine equity investments, capacity-purchase commitments and contingent lease support, noting that residual-value guarantees typically have caps and protect investors only in part.22

What Not to Assume About the $63B
  • Do not add it to the roughly $161.2B in Broadcom lease payments or the $42B note facility in the S-1. Funding used to buy equipment may overlap with payments to lease and use that same equipment.
  • Public disclosures do not establish whether this overlaps with June's $35B transaction.
  • Residual-value support is capped. It is not an unconditional guarantee of all debt.
  • The banks arranging the loans may not be the institutions that ultimately retain the exposure.

Hyperscalers Turn to Corporate Bonds

Debt issuance is already visible in completed offerings. One example is Microsoft's senior unsecured bonds, priced on October 29.

  • Size $25B across ten maturities running from 2028 to 2066.
  • Coupons 5.55% on the 2036 notes; 6.35% on the 2066 notes.
  • Use of proceeds General corporate purposes, including capex. It would be wrong to assume every dollar finances AI data centers.23

Highly rated companies can still raise enormous sums. The terms are far removed from those on CoreWeave's bonds discussed below.

Estimates of future corporate bond issuance differ by more than a factor of two.

  • iM Securities It estimates hyperscaler capex has reached roughly 100% of operating cash flow, versus a 10-year average of 40%. Bond issuance had already reached $194B by July 7, surpassing the $108B issued in all of 2025. iM forecasts $250B in 2026 and $400B in 2027.8
  • Eugene Investment & Securities Analyst Heo Jae-hwan estimates $840B of 2027 bond issuance by the Big Four—Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta—equivalent to 40% of net U.S. Treasury issuance.24

The gap reflects different coverage (all hyperscalers versus the Big Four) and assumptions (Eugene assumes 70% debt financing of $1.2 trillion in capex).

Hyperscalers' share of bond markets has also grown.

  • BIS In 2026, hyperscaler bonds with maturities of ten years or longer accounted for about 24% of corporate bond issuance at comparable maturities and 13% of gross U.S. Treasury issuance; the respective shares in 2024 were about 3% and 1.5%.22
  • IMF Hyperscalers account for roughly 10% of global investment-grade corporate issuance.5

So far, however, the measured market impact has been limited. The BIS analyzed 18 days in 2026 with AI financing news and found that the U.S. 10-year term premium—the additional yield investors demand for holding longer-dated bonds—rose on average about 1.5 basis points (0.015 percentage points), then reversed within two days. Investment-grade credit spreads did not move materially. The BIS cautioned that sustained issuance at this pace could increase long-term yield pressure and financial-stability risks.22

Hanwha Investment & Securities decomposed the 168-basis-point rise in the U.S. 10-year yield, from 3.63% in September 2024 to 5.31% in October 2026, and attributed 74% of the increase to the term premium.25 That decomposition does not establish what portion, if any, was caused by AI bond issuance.

Debt Beyond the Bond Market

In an August article, University of Chicago Booth professor Stefan Hepp divided AI infrastructure borrowing into five categories (below). Three findings matter most.

  • Total: $1.03T–$1.065T The range depends on whether GPU-backed lending is already included in private credit. The approximately $200B private-credit figure is the author's estimate, not a directly observed total.
  • First losses sit outside banks Most first-loss exposure sits outside the regulated banking system, and debt maturities are relatively long. In his view, this makes a repeat of 2008 unlikely.
  • Stress-scenario losses: $60B–$140B These are modeled realized credit losses under a severe repricing of AI-related assets—not a forecast of what will actually be lost.26, 27

iM Securities estimates off-balance-sheet SPV borrowing exceeded $120B over the previous 18 months, including $27B in SPV and joint-venture borrowing for Meta's Hyperion data center alone.8

Borrowing Against a Customer's Credit

Kyobo Securities analyst Wi Jae-hyun sees AI financing shifting from unsecured bonds toward collateralized borrowing backed by customers such as hyperscalers, including delayed-draw term loans (DDTLs) and SPV loans.28

Even the same company can face radically different funding costs depending on the collateral package. CoreWeave's $8.5B secured facility, signed in late March, carries a floating rate of SOFR (the U.S. short-term reference rate) plus 2.25% and an approximately 5.9% fixed-rate tranche. Moody's assigned the loan an A3 rating. That rating applies to the facility secured by subsidiary assets and specific customer contracts—not to CoreWeave as a company.29 By contrast, the secondary-market yield on CoreWeave's unsecured notes due 2031 briefly reached 16% in October.30

“Who repays?” has two answers: where the money comes from (the source of repayment) and who is legally responsible—and absorbs losses—if it does not arrive (the allocation of liability).

Funding Sources: Cash on Hand and Future Inflows

The sources of capital available to Anthropic differ in their degree of certainty.

  • Cash and investments on hand $131.7B in cash and short-term investments as of June 30 (excluding a separate $2.5B of restricted cash).
  • Credit facility Expanded a secured facility from $3.25B to $15B in September.
  • Conditional facilities Up to $82B potentially available from Google, Amazon, Broadcom and AMD, subject to the conditions discussed earlier.
  • Potential IPO proceeds Reportedly up to $100B. Proceeds from shares sold by existing shareholders would not go to the company.4, 9

The S-1 states management believes existing cash, cash generated by operations and credit lines will fund at least the next 12 months of working capital, lease payments and capex. That assessment is not a guarantee of safety, but it is the company's own stated near-term liquidity outlook.4

The right question is not, “Can it pay 20 years of commitments in cash today?” It is, “Can it actually secure the cash it needs over the next 12 or 24 months when payments fall due?” The amount payable through 2030—which is crucial to answering that question—is missing from the S-1. Contracts with stated expiration dates end between 2029 (SpaceXAI) and 2036 (Amazon), but some lease and purchase commitments run as long as 20 years.4

Liability: Legal Obligations and Where Losses Fall

Customer payments create a source of repayment, but customers do not legally owe Anthropic's debt. The entities bound by the contracts—and the special-purpose subsidiaries—bear the payment obligations. In a downside scenario, guarantees, collateral and creditor seniority determine who takes the first losses.

The S-1 risk factors also describe mechanisms that can bring obligations forward if things deteriorate.

  • Cross-default Default under certain commercial contracts may trigger cross-default provisions in the credit agreement and some convertible notes, potentially accelerating substantially all outstanding debt.
  • Minimum liquidity The credit agreement requires at least $5B of liquidity starting at year-end Q4 2026. Certain agreements may require the company to reserve up to an additional $10B in cash.4

In short, repayment depends on cash collected from customers and capital raised. If revenue disappoints, losses do not all fall in one place; the contractual waterfall distributes risk among shareholders, junior creditors, suppliers and hyperscalers according to collateral and guarantees. Even when everyone finances the same AI demand, the order of loss depends on whose claim they hold. This is the IMF's wrong-way risk: investment, revenue and collateral exposure are concentrated in one counterparty, so a shock to that counterparty hits several balance sheets at once.5

Signals from Credit Markets

Cho Jun-gi of SK Securities divides credit contagion into two stages. Stage 1 is selective pressure confined to technology and AI borrowers, transmitted to Korea through supply-chain exposures. Stage 2 is a broader credit tightening in which non-tech spreads, primary issuance, refinancing and lending all deteriorate. His October 23 assessment placed the market in Stage 1.10

The ten-year average is a reference point, not a crisis threshold. Spreads can move slightly above average and stabilize, or primary markets can seize up before spreads reach average. Four indicators should be considered together to see whether conditions are shifting toward Stage 2.

  • Speed and persistence How quickly and for how long spreads widen.
  • Breadth Whether stress spreads beyond technology.
  • Primary market access Whether offerings are canceled or refinancing terms worsen.
  • Real-economy effects Whether actual cash flows and contract terms deteriorate.

In BofA's October fund-manager survey, 39% of respondents cited the fallout from hyperscaler capex as the most likely trigger of a credit event—the largest response (as reported by Kiwoom Securities).30

The base cost of funding has not come down either. On October 28 (early October 29 in Korea), the Federal Reserve held its policy-rate target range at 3.75%–4.00%. The vote was 10–2, with dissenters Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack favoring a 25-basis-point increase.41 This was no signal of imminent relief in borrowing costs. The longer collection takes, the more interest and lease obligations accumulate in the meantime.

Conclusion

AI circular financing is real. But its existence does not, by itself, mean that the revenue is fictitious or collapse is imminent. Anthropic's customer revenue increased 14.7-fold in a year, and operating cash flow turned positive. Yet the infrastructure and contractual obligations coming due ahead of that revenue also grew 5.6-fold in the six months to June-end, with about $245B of new or amended contracts disclosed afterward. As revenue expanded, so did accounts receivable—the revenue yet to be collected.

What matters is how reliable the capital bridging this timing gap really is, and where losses flow through guarantees and collateral if collections are delayed. The structures disclosed this week trace that exposure through conditional supplier financing, equipment-SPV loans backed by Broadcom residual-value support, and hyperscaler corporate bonds. Korean chipmakers stand to benefit from near-term demand, but their longer-term fortunes remain tied to customers' funding and collections.

Amazon's Q3 results on October 29 gave this mechanism a numerical illustration. AWS revenue rose 39%, yet valuation gains vanished; trailing-12-month free cash flow was −$42.4B, while long-term debt rose $107.5B in nine months. The next tests are the amount payable through 2030 in Anthropic's amended S-1, and whether wider credit spreads spread beyond technology.

Glossary
Circular financing
An arrangement where an investor is also the investee's supplier or customer, allowing invested funds to return to the investor through commercial payments. This report separates customer cash, invested or borrowed capital, and noncash valuation gains.
Hyperscaler
A major technology company that builds and operates vast data-center infrastructure, such as Amazon (AWS), Google, Microsoft or Meta.
Neocloud
A newer cloud provider, such as CoreWeave, Lambda or Nscale, that acquires GPUs and rents out AI compute capacity.
Compute
Processing capacity used to train and serve AI models, encompassing chips, servers, data centers and power.
S-1 · S-1/A
A registration statement filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) before an IPO, and its amended versions. The price range, share count and some initially omitted figures are filled in through amendments.
Vendor financing
Financing in which a seller lends money to, or invests in, a purchaser to help fund the purchase.
NPA
Note Purchase Agreement (NPA): a commitment to purchase convertible notes or equity up to an agreed limit. Anthropic has these arrangements with Google, Amazon and Broadcom.
Convertible notes / fair value
Debt that can be converted to shares at a specified price. A rising company valuation increases the conversion right's value, potentially producing a fair-value loss on the issuer's liability and a gain on the holder's investment.
Noncancelable commitments / shortfall settlements
Contracts requiring minimum payments regardless of usage. If committed usage is not reached, the customer settles the shortfall at the end of the contractual period.
Finance lease
A lease economically similar to financing a purchase. The lessee recognizes both an asset and a liability on its balance sheet.
SPV
A special-purpose vehicle (SPV) formed to hold particular assets or transactions. It is a legal structure; creating an SPV does not automatically remove the exposure from consolidated financial statements.
VIE
A variable interest entity (VIE) is an accounting category in which consolidation depends on substantive control and economic exposure rather than equity ownership alone. Anthropic consolidates some leasing entities classified as VIEs. VIE does not automatically mean off-balance-sheet borrowing.
Residual-value support
A commitment to cover a shortfall if leased equipment's value falls below a specified level at lease end or after early termination. Broadcom provides support equal in amount to the equipment SPV's first-lien debt. Such support is typically capped and is not an unconditional payment guarantee.
First-lien / second-lien debt
The repayment order against the same collateral. Senior first-lien lenders recover first; junior second-lien lenders receive what remains, usually absorbing losses sooner in exchange for higher yields.
DDTL
Delayed-draw term loan (DDTL): a term loan drawn in tranches as needed. Neocloud providers can use contracts with hyperscalers as collateral.
Cross-default
A provision under which default on one agreement can trigger default or acceleration under other loans and bonds.
Wrong-way risk
A risk in which the value of an investment in a counterparty falls just as the receivables owed by that counterparty deteriorate. The IMF uses the term when discussing circular financing.
CDS
Credit default swap (CDS): a contract providing default protection, quoted as a premium or spread. A wider CDS spread (in bp) generally signals greater perceived credit risk.
OAS
Option-adjusted spread (OAS): the yield spread of a corporate bond above comparable Treasuries, adjusted for embedded option effects.
Term premium
Compensation for the risk of holding long-term bonds; broadly, the part of long-term yields above the expected path of short-term policy rates.
ATM
At-the-market (ATM) offering: shares sold incrementally at prevailing market prices rather than all at once. The authorized amount is a ceiling, not cash already received.
Forward sale
A trade priced today but settled by delivering shares and collecting cash later. Meta's common-share forward sales are to settle by November 19, 2027.
RPO
Remaining performance obligations (RPO): contracted value for which revenue has not yet been recognized, akin to a measure of backlog.
LTA
Long-term agreement (LTA): a supply contract fixing terms for volumes and prices over several years.
HBM
High-bandwidth memory (HBM): vertically stacked DRAM offering high bandwidth, typically placed beside AI accelerators.
